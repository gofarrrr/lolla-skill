# What Lens A Sees

Inversion sees this move: Ask what would make the plan obviously fail, then look for those conditions now.

Core Principles and Analogies The **fundamental essence** of Inversion is the mental habit of approaching a situation from the opposite end of its natural starting point. Instead of asking how to achieve a desired outcome, inversion demands that you ask what would guarantee failure, and then systematically avoid those things. Core Definition and Purpose Inversion recognizes that it is often much easier to avoid what you don't want than to attain what you do want. The concept was championed by the 19th-century German mathematician Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi, who solved complex elliptical functions by following a simple maxim: **"Man muss immer umkehren"** (Invert, always invert). Jacobi recognized that in the nature of things, many of the hardest problems are best solved only when addressed backward. Charlie Munger elevated inversion from a mathematical technique to a general-purpose think.

# What Lens B Sees

Premortem sees this move: Imagine the plan failed, then name the preventable causes before acting.

Core Principles and Analogies: The **Premortem** is a powerful decision-making technique that deliberately introduces prospective hindsight to mitigate common cognitive biases that lead to project failure. Its fundamental essence is to force participants to **look backward from a simulated future failure** to identify its causes. The core purpose of the Premortem is to transcend the inherent limitations of the **inside view**—which is shaped by personal beliefs and experiences—by adopting the **outside view**—how the situation looks independent of one's own perspective. This method is crucial because many common cognitive biases are products of the inside view, and simply being smarter does not make one immune; in fact, being smart can sometimes worsen motivated reasoning because clever people can spin better narratives to defend their point of view. # What They Correct In Each Other

Plain teaching: the pair is useful as a sequence. Inversion turns the desired outcome upside down; Premortem turns that backward view into a concrete failure-finding exercise.

If you use only Inversion, the warning can stay abstract. If you use only Premortem, the exercise can become a checklist without a clear reversal. Together, the move is: turn the goal upside down, then inspect the case for conditions that would make that failure real.

# When This Pair Misleads

Common Failure Modes and Cognitive Biases 1. **Illusion of Control and Overoptimism:** The most common blind spot when failing to apply inversion. When humans only plan forward, they fall in love with their own ideas and become blind to disconfirming evidence, assuming their path to success will be friction-free. 2. **The Plunging-In Bias:** Starting to solve a problem before understanding the forces holding the problem in place. Inversion corrects this by insisting you map the failure landscape before designing the solution. 3. **Fundamental Attribution Error in Failure Analysis:** When asking "how would this fail?", teams might incorrectly attribute hypothetical failure to external, uncontrollable circumstances rather than looking at internal, controllable inputs — producing an inversion exercise that feels rigorous but avoids accountability. 4. **The Echo Chamber of Negativity:** Ove.
