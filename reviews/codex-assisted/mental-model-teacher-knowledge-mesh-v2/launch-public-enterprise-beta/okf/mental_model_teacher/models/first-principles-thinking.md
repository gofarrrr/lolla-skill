# First Principles Thinking

# Core Doctrine

Core Principles and Analogies The **fundamental essence** of First Principles Thinking is to systematically question every assumption underlying a problem, belief, or proposed solution, thereby isolating the most basic, elemental truths from which new knowledge or innovation can be constructed. Core Definition and Purpose First principles thinking strips away assumptions, leaving one solely with a **set of facts and a desired outcome**. The purpose is to **think for yourself** to decide (1) what you want, (2) what is true, and (3) what you should do to achieve your goals in light of the discovered truth. This systematic approach is crucial for navigating complexity and uncovering reality. FPT demands a high degree of **intellectual humility**, requiring thinkers to acknowledge what they do not know and to resist the craving for false pre.

# When To Use

Strengths (Most Powerful Contexts) FPT is most effective in scenarios demanding radical clarity, invention, and systemic overhaul: • **Most useful when** existing categories, market assumptions, or inherited rules are distorting the problem rather than clarifying it. • **Works best when** the team needs to separate facts from conventions before deciding what is actually non-negotiable. • **Best used when** a novel solution requires rebuilding the argument from elemental truths instead of optimizing a stale template. • **Genuinely Novel Problems:** When encountering problems of real consequence that defy conventional solutions, FPT is essential. It helps avoid substitution bias, where a known but unsuitable simple model is applied instead of understanding the more complicated actual model. • **Overcoming Outdated Paradigms:** It is crucia.

# When Not To Use

Weaknesses and Anti-Patterns • **Danger when** “first principles” is used as a status move to ignore practical constraints, accumulated know-how, or real implementation costs. • **Cognitive Load and Time:** FPT is slow, conscious, and demanding work, engaging the methodical, analytical parts of the brain (System 2). It is mentally taxing and requires time to "gestate". • **The "Boil the Ocean" Trap:** A dangerous anti-pattern is excessive fact-gathering and analysis, a practice known as "boiling the ocean". FPT requires structuring the problem first with a hypothesis and gathering **only enough facts** to prove or disprove that hypothesis—anything more is wasteful. • **The Flawed Hero:** In the corporate setting, the inherent challenge of FPT is psychological. # Dangerous Misuse

Common Failure Modes and Biases 1. **Anchoring and Framing Biases:** FPT starts by choosing the fundamental concepts, but this framing itself is a trap. The **anchoring bias** occurs when a thinker focuses excessively on a single piece of initial information, making them ignorant of the rest of the picture. Similarly, the way a situation is *framed* can influence how it is perceived, potentially leading to immediate rejection if framed poorly (e.g., as excessive resource use instead of opportunity). 2. **Confirmation Bias and Groupthink:** The intense mental work of FPT makes thinkers fiercely defensive of their resulting analysis. **Confirmation bias** causes the suppression of assumptions and reluctance to embrace new information that contradicts the initial principles chosen. In a team setting, this leads to **groupthink**, especially.
