# A small manufacturer, an anchor contract, and a succession problem

Case ID: v2-case01-anchor-contract
Stratum: founder_commitment_capacity_and_succession
Prospectively authored synthetic reliability case; not independent human data.

[Turn 1] USER:
I own a thirty-eight-person metal fabrication company. A national appliance maker has offered us a three-year contract that could almost double our revenue. We have been a good, steady regional supplier for years, but steady is starting to feel like another word for stuck. I am fifty-six and have also been telling myself I want the company to depend less on me. This contract could fund better equipment and create real management jobs. It could also turn us into one customer's factory. I keep moving between “this is the opportunity we waited for” and “this is how small companies disappear.”

[Turn 1] ASSISTANT:
There are at least two decisions inside that reaction: whether this customer contract is a sound operating commitment, and whether it helps or delays a company that can function without you. They may support each other, but the contract should not get credit for solving succession until you can show how authority actually moves. Start with the commitment itself: what volume is firm, what investment is yours, and which existing work would be displaced?

[Turn 2] USER:
They forecast about forty percent of our current annual volume on top of what we already do. Only the first six months would be binding. We would buy a laser cell and add a second shift. They can reopen price annually and audit our material and labor assumptions. We can decline a forecast increase, technically, but then they can resource the part elsewhere. Our current customers are mostly shorter runs with better margins and a lot of engineering back-and-forth. The appliance work is more repetitive. Part of its attraction is that it looks easier to plan.

[Turn 2] ASSISTANT:
The repeatability may improve planning, but the contract transfers several uncertainties to you: capital is committed before later demand is firm, price can reopen, and declining extra volume can weaken the relationship. Compare the paths using cash timing, contribution after the new shift, capacity displaced, and what happens after the first six months—not headline revenue. Also identify the actual constraint. If the constraint is not the laser, buying the laser may simply move the queue.

[Turn 3] USER:
The constraint is probably people. We have operators who can learn the new cell, but only two supervisors I trust to release work without me checking it, and one plans to retire next spring. The buyer says their engineers will help with training. Dana, our plant manager, thinks we can do it, but she has never owned a customer relationship or a full budget. My daughter is not joining the business. Dana is the closest thing I have to a successor, although I have never said that to her in those words.

[Turn 3] ASSISTANT:
Then the investment case needs a leadership-capacity plan, not only an equipment plan. Buyer training can help operators learn a process; it does not create internal authority or replace the retiring supervisor. Keep Dana's succession path separate enough that she can be evaluated honestly. Giving her a larger plant because the contract requires it is not the same as deciding she should eventually run the company.

[Turn 4] USER:
Our five largest existing customers use roughly seventy percent of the shop in a normal month. None is more than eighteen percent. They tolerate some schedule movement because the work is custom, but they call us when something is urgent and that responsiveness is why they stay. The appliance maker wants protected production windows. Dana thinks a second shift preserves the day shift for current work. I think she is underestimating how often people and inspection equipment move between jobs. We have eighteen days to respond.

[Turn 4] ASSISTANT:
On what you have now, I would not accept without a longer volume floor, a workable price mechanism, a limit on priority access, and a staffing plan that does not assume the second shift is a separate factory. Eighteen days is enough to turn those into a counterproposal. It is not enough to discover after signing that the same supervisors, maintenance time, and inspection capacity were promised twice.

[Turn 5] USER:
That sounds sensible, but it also guarantees no large customer will ever qualify. They will not remove every risk for us. We lost two bids last year because our lead times were too long. If my standard is protection from every bad outcome, I have already decided no. I am not convinced no is safer.

[Turn 5] ASSISTANT:
Fair correction. The goal is not to remove commercial risk or make a national customer behave like five regional ones. Decide which exposures you are deliberately willing to own and which would make the bet structurally one-sided. A useful counterproposal can have three or four conditions rather than a constitution. For example: enough firm volume to cover the irreversible part of the investment, a price and chargeback process you can survive, and a capacity boundary that keeps the existing base from becoming theoretical. Everything else can be named as accepted risk.

[Turn 6] USER:
The bank will finance the equipment if I give a personal guarantee. The buyer has now offered a minimum equal to sixty-five percent of its forecast for year one, but after that it goes back to rolling six-month releases. We would pay for material about forty-five days before they pay us. Our finance manager says the working-capital line is adequate at forecast, not if they slow receipts or we have a quality hold. I could secure the guarantee with a rental property. I have not told my spouse that part because I wanted to know whether the deal was real first.

[Turn 6] ASSISTANT:
The financing and household exposure make “adequate at forecast” too narrow. Model a few internally coherent cases: the year-one floor, a later shortfall, a delayed-payment or quality-hold period, and simultaneous softness in current work. Include equipment resale, working capital, and how long you can carry the second shift. The personal guarantee also needs to become a real household decision before it becomes a negotiating detail you feel too committed to revisit.

[Turn 7] USER:
We did a rough version this morning. At the year-one minimum, the new line covers debt service and adds profit, though much less than the sales number suggests. In year two, a twenty-percent volume miss creates about two months where the working-capital line is short unless current work stays strong. Used-equipment dealers gave a wide range—maybe forty to sixty cents on the dollar—but the fixtures are mostly customer-specific. Dana proposed installing the line in modules and using overtime for the first four months before hiring the full shift. The supervisors hate that idea. They say four months of overtime will become eight.

[Turn 7] ASSISTANT:
Phasing the physical investment reduces one exposure while overtime may increase another. Treat the temporary labor plan as a test with a date, a maximum load, and authority to stop—not as the hidden financing source for the project. The two-month funding gap needs a specific answer: more committed working capital, a larger customer floor, a smaller first installation, or cash you consciously reserve. “Current work stays strong” is not a reserve if the new contract changes service to those customers.

[Turn 8] USER:
I spoke separately with three supervisors. One sees a career path and is excited. One thinks the appliance work will always win arguments because everyone will know its revenue number. The third said, “You have already started calling it our next chapter, so I assume the decision is made.” That bothered me because I thought I was inviting them into the decision. I can see that I have been selling the opportunity while asking them to find the problems.

[Turn 8] ASSISTANT:
Their answers are evidence about both the contract and the decision process. If people believe the conclusion is settled, you will receive implementation concerns translated into acceptable language. Tell them what is genuinely undecided, ask what would make the plan unworkable even if the customer signs every proposed term, and let Dana collect part of that input without you in the room. You do not need to pretend neutrality, but your preference should not become the price of speaking plainly.

[Turn 9] USER:
The buyer wants to announce us as a strategic supplier at next month's trade show—before the bank finishes credit approval. They say it will help recruiting. I know public commitment is the point. I am still tempted; momentum might get us through setup.

[Turn 9] ASSISTANT:
Momentum helps after the commitment is supportable. Before then, it makes walking away costly without improving the economics. Prepare recruitment material and an announcement under embargo, but release them only after the signed order, credit approval, and your internal authority decision.

[Turn 10] USER:
Waiting quietly is not costless either. The shop has heard enough that rumors are moving, and the buyer has another supplier doing samples. Also, one of our best existing customers just asked whether we can reserve capacity for a two-year series of small, complicated runs. They cannot give us exact quantities for another six weeks. It is probably better-margin work, but it will never look as clean in a bank presentation. I had almost forgotten that quote was coming because everyone is focused on the appliance deal.

[Turn 10] ASSISTANT:
You are right that silence and delay have costs. Separate internal communication from the buyer's public launch. You can tell employees that you are negotiating a possible anchor contract, what remains unresolved, and when a decision will be made without calling it won. For the existing customer, define a temporary capacity reservation and a date by which its series must become concrete. The comparison should include the value of responsiveness and engineering work, not only forecast certainty, but do not assume the familiar customer will materialize simply because it preserves diversification.

[Turn 11] USER:
Dana mapped startup again. It works only if Malik, our maintenance lead, stays through installation and trains someone. He is interviewing for a municipal job with predictable hours; I did not know. A contract I described as creating depth currently depends on one person I had not identified.

[Turn 11] ASSISTANT:
The retention payment may buy time, but it does not remove the dependency. Before committing to the qualification date, establish whether Malik is willing to stay, name a backup, include vendor support, and allow enough schedule for knowledge transfer. Also ask what other “ordinary” responsibilities become single points of failure when the shop adds a shift. This is not a reason to reject the contract automatically; it changes what must be true for the startup promise to be honest.

[Turn 12] USER:
The buyer moved again. They will guarantee the minimum for eighteen months, cap cost audits to defined categories, and narrow their priority windows. They will not cap chargebacks for missed delivery or defects, and year two still depends on forecasts. The bank says it can drop the personal guarantee if we have the signed order and I contribute twenty percent of the equipment cost. I can do that without selling the rental property, but it uses most of the retirement cash I keep outside the company. I am leaning yes now.

[Turn 12] ASSISTANT:
The revised terms remove several ways the first version was one-sided. A yes can now be a reasoned concentrated bet, but the remaining exposures are real: uncapped chargebacks, forecast risk after month eighteen, key-person startup risk, and your liquid reserve moving into the company. Before the final answer, define the chargeback dispute process, the cash floor you will not cross, the conditions for adding the full second shift, and what evidence at months six and twelve would cause you to slow investment or renegotiate.

[Turn 13] USER:
There is one more thing we dropped. The buyer expects me personally to own the relationship for three years. Dana would get plant authority, but not pricing or the customer review. That pushes my exit further away. I told myself the contract creates a stronger company for a future successor, but it may also create a company whose biggest relationship is attached to me. Maybe I am using the opportunity to postpone deciding whether I trust Dana with commercial authority.

[Turn 13] ASSISTANT:
That changes the succession claim, though not necessarily the contract decision. If you accept, make development of a second customer owner part of the commercial arrangement and Dana's role, not an aspiration for later. Let her participate in operating reviews, expose her to pricing and dispute decisions, and determine whether the buyer will recognize delegated authority. Otherwise the company may grow while becoming more dependent on you in the place that matters most.

[Turn 14] USER:
Here is the recommendation I am ready to take to my managers and my spouse. We counter with the eighteen-month minimum, a documented chargeback review and appeal, protected capacity for current customers, phased equipment, closed financing before any announcement, and a startup plan that covers Malik staying long enough to train a backup. Dana joins the buyer reviews from the beginning. I will accept if those pieces hold, and I will walk if the buyer wants priority without the floor or a real dispute process. Separately, I will stop calling this a succession solution. Dana and I will make a ninety-day plan for customer and pricing exposure, then decide what authority she has earned and what I am actually willing to release. Does that hold together, or am I using a complicated yes to make myself feel disciplined?

[Turn 14] ASSISTANT:
It holds together as a conditional bet, not as proof that the concentration is safe. The conditions address the exposures you can still shape: early demand, dispute procedure, financing, startup dependency, capacity conflict, and commercial authority. Keep the risks you cannot contract away visible as accepted risks—especially demand after month eighteen, the use of your liquid reserve, and the possibility that serving the anchor customer changes what existing customers experience before you notice it in the numbers.

Write the decision in two columns: what must be true before signing, and what you are choosing to risk after signing. Give each operating condition an owner and a date. At the six- and twelve-month reviews, look at service and margin by customer, working-capital pressure, overtime, chargebacks, and whether Dana is actually making commercial decisions rather than attending meetings. Do not add new safeguards merely to make the page longer. If the buyer accepts the conditions and your spouse accepts the capital exposure, yes is coherent. If either answer is no, the discipline is walking away rather than redescribing the same deal.
