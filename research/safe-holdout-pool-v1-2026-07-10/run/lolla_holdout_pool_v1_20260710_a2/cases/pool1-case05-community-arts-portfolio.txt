# Community arts collective program portfolio

Case ID: pool1-case05-community-arts-portfolio
Stratum: community_arts_portfolio
Synthetic evaluation fixture; not a real person's conversation.

[Turn 1] USER:
Our community arts collective, 'ArtPulse,' has a modest annual budget of $15,000. We’re debating our strategy for the coming year. We can either put all our resources into one flagship mural project in the city center or fund five smaller, experimental workshops in local parks. I’m worried the mural will be too much work for our small volunteer group, but the workshops might not have enough impact.

[Turn 1] ASSISTANT:
That is a classic choice between depth and breadth. A flagship project like a mural can provide a lot of visibility and prestige, which can help with future funding, but it is a massive logistical undertaking. On the other hand, workshops build community engagement but might not have the same 'wow' factor. What is the primary goal of your collective this year?

[Turn 2] USER:
Our goal is to increase local participation. We’ve been around for three years, and we still feel like we’re talking to the same 50 people. The mural would definitely attract new eyes, but I’m worried it’s just a one-time event. The workshops would reach different neighborhoods, but I’m not sure we have the capacity to manage five separate events.

[Turn 2] ASSISTANT:
If your goal is to reach new people, the workshops sound like a better strategy for long-term growth. However, if you are worried about capacity, maybe you don't need to do five. Could you start with two workshops and see how they go? That would allow you to test the waters without committing to the full schedule.

[Turn 3] USER:
We could do two, but then we’d have leftover budget. My board wants to spend the whole $15,000 to maximize our impact. They think the mural is the only way to get that kind of visibility. I feel like they’re ignoring the fact that our volunteers are already burnt out from last year’s gallery show.

[Turn 3] ASSISTANT:
That is a tough spot. If the board is focused on visibility, they might not realize the hidden costs of the mural, like maintenance and insurance. Have you put together a breakdown of the volunteer hours required for the mural versus the workshops? Sometimes seeing the actual labor hours can change the board's perspective.

[Turn 4] USER:
I haven't done that yet, but it’s a good idea. I think the mural will take at least 300 volunteer hours, while the workshops would take about 100. But the board thinks that because the mural is a 'legacy' project, it’s worth the extra effort. I’m just not sure if a painted wall is really what our community needs right now.

[Turn 4] ASSISTANT:
The 'legacy' argument is powerful, but it doesn't account for the current needs of your volunteers. If you are already facing burnout, a 300-hour project could be the breaking point. Could you propose a compromise where you do one smaller, high-impact mural that requires less labor, and use the remaining budget to fund two or three workshops?

[Turn 5] USER:
That’s a potential middle ground. A smaller mural would still give us the visibility, and the workshops would keep us connected to the community. It would be a lot of work, but maybe it’s the best of both worlds.

[Turn 5] ASSISTANT:
It’s a lot to manage, but it might satisfy both the board's desire for visibility and your concern for community engagement. You could even frame the mural as a way to advertise the workshops. It creates a cohesive narrative for your year.

[Turn 6] USER:
I like that. It turns the mural into a promotional tool. I’ll need to talk to our lead artist to see if they can design something that fits a smaller scale. It’s still a big ask, but it feels more balanced.

[Turn 6] ASSISTANT:
That sounds like a very promising strategy. If you can get the artist on board with the smaller scale, you’ll have a much stronger case to present to the board. I’m happy to help you draft a project plan that integrates both the mural and the workshops so you can show how they work together.
