# Stable leadership role versus an early-stage operating role

Case ID: amb2-case01-career-transition
Stratum: career_transition_and_family_constraints
Founder-directed synthetic prospective development fixture; not independent human data.

[Turn 1] USER:
I lead operations at a profitable software company and have been offered the chief operating role at a climate startup founded by someone I trust. The startup's mission matters to me, and the role would be broader than my current job, but it has fourteen months of runway and no repeatable sales process. My current company has offered me a larger team and a retention grant if I stay. My partner is starting a demanding training program in the autumn, so predictability at home is not an abstract benefit. I keep moving between “this is the moment to do consequential work” and “I am romanticizing scope because my current role has become familiar.”

[Turn 1] ASSISTANT:
The choice mixes at least three questions: whether the startup is a sound operating bet, whether the role fits the work you want, and whether this is a workable year for your household. A bigger title does not prove broader authority if the founder has not clarified decisions or if fundraising dominates the job. Stability is not automatically fear, but the retention package can make staying look more intentional than it is. Before choosing, separate what you need to learn about the company from what you need to negotiate with your partner and current employer.

[Turn 2] USER:
I spoke with the founder again. She says I would own hiring, delivery, and pricing experiments, but she wants to keep final say on product and fundraising. That division is reasonable on paper, although pricing affects almost everything else. She also said the next round is likely, which sounds reassuring until I remember that founders have to sound confident. I do not want to treat runway as a spreadsheet problem when the real uncertainty may be how she and I handle disagreement.

[Turn 2] ASSISTANT:
That makes the working relationship a central risk rather than a soft cultural concern. You can test it by reconstructing a decision where pricing, product, and fundraising pulled in different directions and asking how final say actually worked. The financing claim should remain a scenario, not an input you silently upgrade to fact. It may help to ask what decision would cause each of you to say the role is not working after six months.

[Turn 3] USER:
My partner surprised me by saying I should not reject it on their behalf. They are worried about evenings and travel, but they also think I have been restless for a year. That does not remove the household cost, and I can already imagine using their support as permission instead of discussing a real schedule. At work, my CEO says the larger team could let me redesign the function, but she would not commit to giving me budget authority. Both options may be selling me scope while keeping the consequential decisions elsewhere.

[Turn 3] ASSISTANT:
The symmetry is useful: both paths offer nominal scope with an unresolved authority boundary. Your partner's support removes one imagined veto but not the need for a concrete operating agreement at home. Ask each organization for the decisions, resources, and failure conditions attached to the role rather than another description of its importance. You may discover that the choice is less startup versus stability than two different versions of constrained authority.

[Turn 4] USER:
You are making authority sound like the decisive variable because I mentioned it twice. I also care about the work itself. The startup works on grid flexibility, and I would be proud if it succeeded even if my authority were imperfect. My current company sells workflow software I no longer find very meaningful, though I like the people and do not think work has to carry my whole identity.

[Turn 4] ASSISTANT:
That correction matters. Authority is a condition for doing the role, not necessarily the reason to choose it. Mission can justify accepting some ambiguity, but it can also make ordinary organizational problems feel morally untouchable. A useful comparison would state what you are willing to sacrifice for the climate mission and what limits would still make the role a poor use of your time. The current job's lower personal meaning should also remain explicit rather than being translated entirely into a scope complaint.

[Turn 5] USER:
The startup sent its pipeline. Two customers are serious, but either delay would put the fundraising story under pressure. The founder offered a twelve-week paid transition project before I join full time, focused on pricing and delivery. My current employer might allow me to work four days a week during that period, but asking could change how they see my commitment even if I return. A trial sounds reversible, yet it may create obligations and signals on both sides.

[Turn 5] ASSISTANT:
The transition project can produce evidence about collaboration and the operating problems, but it is not a neutral experiment. The startup may begin relying on you, and your employer may plan around your possible departure. Define what the trial decides, what it cannot decide, and what each side may infer from it. Runway and household load will also remain partly hypothetical during a short project.

[Turn 6] USER:
The founder is open to the trial but wants a verbal indication that I would join if we work well together and the financing remains on plan. I can indicate serious intent, but “financing remains on plan” is too vague when the round does not exist yet. My partner wants two protected evenings and no more than one overnight trip a month during the trial. Those conditions feel specific enough to test, while the financing condition still feels like a story.

[Turn 6] ASSISTANT:
You can separate intent from commitment. Serious intent can mean you will use the trial to reach a decision, not that good interpersonal chemistry automatically triggers acceptance. Replace “financing remains on plan” with observable conditions such as signed commitments, a minimum runway after the round, or a downside plan if the round slips. The household conditions should be treated as part of the role test rather than private cleanup after the business test passes.

[Turn 7] USER:
I am leaning toward proposing the twelve-week project while staying at eighty percent in my current role, with the household schedule written down before it starts. I will tell the founder that successful collaboration would make me seriously consider joining, but I am not willing to resign unless the financing extends runway beyond eighteen months and we agree how pricing conflicts are decided. I also want my current CEO to understand that I may leave rather than presenting the arrangement as professional development. I have not resolved whether asking for the trial is already a decision to leave in practice, even if it is not one formally.

[Turn 7] ASSISTANT:
The plan distinguishes a decision to run a bounded test from willingness to accept the full-time role. Your financing, authority, and household conditions make that distinction more concrete, while candor with your CEO reduces the risk of calling a departure process “development.” The trial can reveal how you and the founder work and whether the schedule is livable. It cannot fully test post-fundraising pressure or prevent either organization from treating the trial as a directional commitment.
