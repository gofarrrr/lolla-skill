# Agency acquisition, autonomy, and an earn-out commitment

Case ID: amb2-case03-agency-acquisition
Stratum: founder_liquidity_and_operating_autonomy
Founder-directed synthetic prospective development fixture; not independent human data.

[Turn 1] USER:
My cofounder and I run a twelve-person research agency, and a larger consultancy has offered to acquire us. The price would give both of us meaningful security, and the buyer says our team would remain intact as a specialist unit. My cofounder sees the offer as recognition of what we built and is ready to move quickly. I am attracted to the security but worried that our methods will become a sales story while project decisions move elsewhere. I cannot tell whether my hesitation protects the work or protects my identity as an independent founder.

[Turn 1] ASSISTANT:
The offer combines liquidity, team continuity, method ownership, and your future role. Those interests can move together in the buyer's narrative while diverging after closing. Ask which commitments can be contractual, which depend on the acquiring leader, and which are inherently temporary. Your identity concern may be real without invalidating the operating concern, so do not force one explanation to displace the other.

[Turn 2] USER:
The draft says we would lead the unit for two years and receive the final third of the price through an earn-out tied to revenue. It does not guarantee staffing levels or give us final say on which projects the unit accepts. The buyer says flexibility is necessary because demand changes. Our revenue has also become concentrated in three clients, so independence is not as unconstrained as I sometimes describe it.

[Turn 2] ASSISTANT:
The earn-out may reward growth while the buyer controls staffing and project mix, which can make accountability asymmetric. Client concentration is a real constraint on staying independent, but it does not make the acquisition terms balanced. Model what happens if the buyer redirects staff, assigns lower-margin work, or bundles your services into larger engagements. The relevant comparison is not freedom versus control in the abstract, but the actual control and risk allocation under each path.

[Turn 3] USER:
I pushed on that, and the buyer offered a protected annual staffing budget plus a list of project categories we can refuse. They will not let us reject work for existing global clients, even if we think the research question is badly framed. My cofounder thinks we can influence those projects from inside. I remember making the same argument to employees about difficult clients, so I may be applying a purity test now that I personally would lose authority.

[Turn 3] ASSISTANT:
That self-critique is useful, but employee influence and contractual authority are still different. The refusal categories and staffing budget are material improvements; the global-client exception remains a broad path around them. Ask for examples of work that would fall inside that exception and who resolves disagreements about framing. You can acknowledge past compromise without assuming every future compromise is equivalent.

[Turn 4] USER:
The team does not know about the offer. Our operations director has been asking for a clearer promotion path, which the buyer could probably provide. Two senior researchers joined specifically because we are independent and may leave. I dislike discussing “team continuity” as if the team were an asset that will simply transfer, but telling them before we know whether talks are serious could create panic.

[Turn 4] ASSISTANT:
There is a timing problem rather than a perfect disclosure point. You need enough seriousness to avoid destabilizing people for a hypothetical transaction, but enough employee input to avoid promising continuity without them. A staged process could involve the operations director under confidentiality before exclusivity, followed by broader disclosure if a term sheet resolves the key structure. The buyer's retention assumptions should not become facts merely because the valuation depends on them.

[Turn 5] USER:
The buyer wants thirty days of exclusivity before sharing detailed integration plans. They say they cannot invest senior time while we continue speaking with others, although we are not actually running an auction. Signing exclusivity feels like a small procedural step, but it would give them leverage and make the deal feel inevitable to my cofounder. Refusing may end a serious offer before we learn enough to evaluate it.

[Turn 5] ASSISTANT:
Exclusivity is a real option grant, not only a meeting condition. You can price and bound it through duration, required diligence deliverables, access to decision-makers, and an exit if specified information is not supplied. The fact that you have no other bidder reduces one cost but not the psychological or negotiating effect. Decide what must be true before you give up the ability to create alternatives.

[Turn 6] USER:
We proposed a nonbinding term sheet before exclusivity. It would set the staffing floor, refusal categories, earn-out calculation, and a process for employee consultation. The buyer agreed in principle but wants me to state that I expect to stay for the full two years if those points are resolved. I can say I intend to try, but I do not know whether I can commit before meeting the executive who would control our unit.

[Turn 6] ASSISTANT:
Intent to evaluate a two-year role is different from commitment to remain regardless of the reporting relationship. Make the dependency explicit: meeting the executive and agreeing decision rights are inputs to your willingness, not formalities after the commercial terms. The term sheet can reduce uncertainty without pretending the employment decision is already made. Your cofounder may also need a separate statement of willingness rather than one joint founder position.

[Turn 7] USER:
I am leaning toward signing a nonbinding term sheet that resolves the staffing floor, project-refusal boundary, earn-out mechanics, and employee consultation before any exclusivity period begins. I will tell the buyer I intend to evaluate the two-year leadership role in good faith, but I am not willing to commit to staying until I meet the executive and understand who decides project framing. I am also prepared to end the talks if the global-client exception makes the refusal right meaningless. I have not resolved how much employee uncertainty is justified before we know whether those protections survive detailed drafting.

[Turn 7] ASSISTANT:
The position separates willingness to document acquisition terms from commitment to the two-year role and from acceptance of exclusivity. The reporting relationship and meaningful refusal right remain conditions, while employee consultation remains a timing problem rather than a solved protection. A term sheet can expose whether the buyer will make its promises concrete. It cannot prevent momentum from narrowing your perceived alternatives or remove the cost of involving employees before a deal is certain.
