# Permanent community space versus a bounded occupancy test

Case ID: amb2-case02-community-space
Stratum: community_space_and_financial_commitment
Founder-directed synthetic prospective development fixture; not independent human data.

[Turn 1] USER:
I help run a neighborhood arts group that has borrowed classrooms and church halls for six years. A landlord has offered us a visible storefront on a five-year lease at below-market rent, but even the discounted rent would use almost half our unrestricted budget. Several teaching artists want a permanent home because moving materials is exhausting and participants never know where to find us. Our treasurer thinks a lease could turn the group into a building-maintenance organization. I want the stability and visibility, but I cannot tell whether the space would serve the program or slowly become the program.

[Turn 1] ASSISTANT:
The storefront could solve real access and coordination problems while creating a fixed cost that changes what the organization must prioritize. Below-market rent is not automatically affordable if it consumes flexibility or requires growth to sustain it. Identify which benefits require a permanent address and which could come from better scheduling, storage, or a shorter agreement. The governance question matters too: who can reduce programming or raise money when the building demands it?

[Turn 2] USER:
We mapped the current costs, and storage, transport, and last-minute rentals already equal about a third of the proposed rent. That makes the lease look less dramatic. But volunteers absorb much of the moving work without invoicing us, so the comparison is partly about whether we count their exhaustion. The storefront would also let us run daytime programs, although we do not yet have instructors committed for those hours.

[Turn 2] ASSISTANT:
Counting hidden labor makes the status quo more expensive, but potential daytime use should not be booked as existing demand. Separate savings you can verify from capacity you hope to create. A lease may be justified by reducing current friction even if growth never appears, but then the budget must work on that basis. It would also help to ask volunteers whether a fixed site reduces their load or simply replaces transport work with opening, cleaning, and supervision.

[Turn 3] USER:
The landlord now says he could offer a six-month license with an option to sign the lease afterward. The monthly payment would be higher, and we would have to leave quickly if we decline the lease. Our treasurer likes the shorter commitment. Two teaching artists say another “pilot” will ask them to build a program that the board can abandon just when families begin relying on it.

[Turn 3] ASSISTANT:
The license reduces financial duration but not necessarily community expectation. If you invite families into recurring programs, six months can still create obligations that are costly to unwind. Define the test as narrowly as possible: which uses, attendance patterns, operating tasks, and fundraising signals would inform the lease decision? Also decide what you will tell participants about continuity before they invest in the space.

[Turn 4] USER:
I hear that, but I do not want transparent uncertainty to become an excuse for timid programming. People will not show up to something described as temporary and possibly disappearing. At the same time, promising permanence before the board decides would be dishonest. This is where “community trust” gets used on both sides: either as a reason to commit now or as a reason not to test anything visible.

[Turn 4] ASSISTANT:
Trust does not require pretending uncertainty is absent. It requires being precise about what is promised during the six months and what decision follows. You can offer reliable programming for the licensed period without promising the address indefinitely. The harder issue is whether the board is genuinely willing to sign if the test succeeds, or whether the pilot merely postpones a discomfort it has no criteria to resolve.

[Turn 5] USER:
The board could reserve nine months of rent before the license starts if a pending foundation grant comes through. The program officer says our application is strong but cannot guarantee it. A local business has also offered to sponsor the storefront sign, which is useful but makes me nervous because visibility is becoming easier to fund than teaching. The landlord needs an answer on the license before the grant decision.

[Turn 5] ASSISTANT:
The grant should remain contingent, and sign sponsorship should not be treated as evidence that operating costs are covered. You can decide whether the license is viable without the grant, then specify how the grant changes the lease threshold. The timing may justify a bounded risk, but it should not erase the mismatch between fundable visibility and less visible program labor. A reserve policy can prevent the building from automatically consuming future teaching funds.

[Turn 6] USER:
We drafted criteria: average weekly attendance, volunteer hours, maintenance incidents, and enough unrestricted commitments to cover a year of rent without reducing artist fees. The teaching artists want one more criterion: evidence that families use the space outside headline events. The treasurer wants an automatic no if the foundation grant fails. I think that gives one funder too much control, but I agree we need a financial floor.

[Turn 6] ASSISTANT:
The criteria now cover use, hidden labor, and financial capacity, but they still require a decision rule. The grant can contribute to a funding floor without becoming the floor by itself. Specify the minimum unrestricted reserve and whether pledges count differently from cash. Also decide what happens if community use is strong while the financial threshold is narrowly missed, because that is the conflict most likely to make the criteria disappear after the fact.

[Turn 7] USER:
I am leaning toward accepting the six-month license and running three recurring programs rather than treating the storefront as an open-ended community center. I will recommend the five-year lease only if we finish the license with twelve months of rent committed, stable artist fees, and evidence that regular participants use the space beyond special events. I am willing to close the storefront after six months if those conditions fail, but I am not sure the rest of the board or the teaching artists will accept that after families have formed routines. I still worry that the pilot can measure use and workload without measuring the reputational cost of leaving.

[Turn 7] ASSISTANT:
Your position separates accepting a bounded license from willingness to recommend the long lease. The funding, fee, and use conditions make the later decision inspectable, while the narrow program scope limits some expectation. The unresolved issue is collective willingness to honor an exit rule once real relationships exist. The pilot can reveal use and operating burden; it cannot make departure reputationally neutral or guarantee that the board will follow today's criteria.
