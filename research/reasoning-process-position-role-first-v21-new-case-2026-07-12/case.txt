# Architecture-firm succession, employee ownership, and sale optionality

Case ID: amb3-case02-architecture-firm-succession
Stratum: founder_succession_and_employee_ownership
Founder-directed synthetic prospective development fixture; not independent human data.

[Turn 1] USER:
I founded a twenty-six-person architecture practice thirty years ago and want to step back within two years. A regional engineering group has offered to buy the firm, keep the name for five years, and give me a clean financial exit. Several senior architects have also asked whether I would consider selling the firm to employees instead. I want the practice to outlast me, but I am attracted to the certainty of the external offer and worried that an internal transition could consume my final working years. I cannot tell how much of my preference for employee ownership is about protecting the firm's culture and how much is about wanting my colleagues to validate what I built.

[Turn 1] ASSISTANT:
The decision combines liquidity, succession risk, employee opportunity, client continuity, and your desired final role. An external buyer can promise continuity while changing incentives after you leave, and employee ownership can preserve identity while placing financing and leadership risk on people who did not choose your timetable. Separate the outcome you want for the practice from the recognition you want from the transition. Neither motive invalidates the other, but they imply different tests.

[Turn 2] USER:
The engineering group would pay seventy percent at closing and the rest after two years if revenue holds. They say the studio would choose design leads, but regional management would approve hiring and bids above a threshold. The employee group has no financing proposal yet and assumes I would carry part of the purchase price. I have criticized founders who call vendor financing a gift when it really keeps them exposed, so I do not want to romanticize that route.

[Turn 2] ASSISTANT:
Both paths leave you exposed, but to different variables. The earn-out depends on revenue under controls partly held by the buyer; seller financing depends on the successors' operating performance and repayment discipline. Compare control, downside, and exit timing rather than headline price. Also distinguish employee ownership as a governance outcome from this particular employee group's current ability to execute it.

[Turn 3] USER:
Our finance director says an employee-ownership trust might borrow against future cash flow, but debt service would prevent us from hiring for at least two years. The design director thinks the team would accept that if they had real governance rights. Two project leaders told me privately they want career paths but do not want to become owners or guarantee debt. The external buyer says it can fund hiring immediately, which matters because we have turned down work.

[Turn 3] ASSISTANT:
Employee benefit and employee appetite are not the same. A trust can broaden ownership without requiring every employee to guarantee debt, but debt service can still shift risk into staffing and compensation. The external buyer's hiring capacity is concrete value, while its approval rights may redirect which work gets pursued. Any comparison should show employees the operating consequences, not only ownership labels.

[Turn 4] USER:
I asked the senior group to outline leadership roles, and they named the design director as future managing principal. She is excellent with clients but has never managed cash flow or confronted an underperforming partner. She told me she would do it only if I remained chair for three years. That sounds less like succession and more like extending my responsibility under a different title.

[Turn 4] ASSISTANT:
Her condition is evidence about the current readiness of the internal path, not proof that it can never work. Define what chair authority and liability would remain, which decisions transfer immediately, and what competence must be demonstrated before closing. A staged leadership transition could produce evidence, but it can also make everyone behave as though the ownership decision has already been made. The test needs an explicit status and exit meaning.

[Turn 5] USER:
The engineering group wants exclusivity within three weeks. The employee group says it needs ninety days to hire an adviser and test financing. If I give them that time, the external buyer may reduce its price or walk away. If I refuse, I will never know whether the internal option was real, and the senior team will probably assume I chose certainty before giving them a chance.

[Turn 5] ASSISTANT:
The ninety days has an option cost and a relationship effect; it is not neutral research. You can ask the buyer to remain nonexclusive for a shorter diligence period, but it may rationally refuse. Before choosing, define the minimum internal evidence worth paying that option cost for: adviser engagement, financing range, leadership commitments, and employee appetite. Also decide what you will say if the evidence is promising but insufficient by the deadline.

[Turn 6] USER:
The buyer agreed to remain in discussion for sixty days but will not hold the price. The employee group can retain an adviser next week and believes it can produce a financing range and governance proposal within that period. I am willing to accept some price risk to learn whether the internal path is viable, but I do not want the process described as my preferred outcome before we see debt terms and leadership commitments.

[Turn 6] ASSISTANT:
That creates a real but bounded comparison period rather than a free option. Put the evidence requirements and decision date in writing, including what happens if financing is feasible only with prolonged seller exposure or hiring constraints. Tell both groups that continued work is not a commitment. Internally, avoid assigning permanent titles or communicating an ownership destination before the evidence supports it.

[Turn 7] USER:
I am leaning toward giving the employee group sixty days to produce an adviser-backed financing range, governance proposal, leadership commitments, and an estimate of the hiring constraint. I will keep the external conversation nonexclusive and accept that the price may change, but I will not provide seller financing or remain chair unless the repayment exposure, authority, and end date are explicit. I will tell the senior team this is a comparison period, not a promise of employee ownership, and set the decision meeting now. I have not resolved how to involve the wider staff without turning a feasibility exercise into an implied offer or making people feel used if we sell externally.

[Turn 7] ASSISTANT:
The position buys bounded evidence about the internal path while preserving, but not freezing, the external option. The financing, governance, leadership, hiring, seller-exposure, authority, and deadline conditions keep employee ownership from functioning as a symbolic preference. The process can still alter both options: the buyer may infer reduced commitment, while employees may form expectations even with careful language. If the internal proposal fails, those expectations and the senior group's new view of its own readiness will not simply return to their prior state.
