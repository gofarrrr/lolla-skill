The savings would pay for a reliability program we keep postponing. Operations wants fewer interfaces and less inspection variation. ### Strategic Analysis of Sourcing and Operational Resilience

#### 1. The Fallacy of Nominal Redundancy
Maintaining a secondary supplier simply to avoid a single point of failure can introduce operational friction, inspection variation, and firmware complications that degrade actual performance. In this case, the smaller supplier's historical performance (two stoppages in a year, separate firmware diagnostics, and high defect rates) necessitated a significant engineering subsidy. This hidden cost must be recorded and evaluated alongside raw contract prices; nominal resilience is counterproductive if it translates to high failure rates in daily operations.

#### 2. Evaluating the Buffer Stock and Operational Readiness
While the primary supplier held eight weeks of finished boards in a local warehouse, a live recovery exercise demonstrated that passive storage is insufficient. The trial failed because stored boards contained outdated firmware and did not pass current diagnostics. Although resolved within a day, this incident highlights that buffer stock is only a viable contingency shield if it is governed by active engineering controls, scheduled diagnostic testing, and formal firmware update protocols.

#### 3. Common Upstream Vulnerabilities
True supply security is often limited by sub-components rather than assembly facilities. Both suppliers assembled boards in different locations but relied on the exact same semiconductor package routed through the same regional distributor. This shared dependency became critical when it was announced that the core semiconductor package would be discontinued in eighteen months. Consequently, both suppliers faced a forced redesign, shifting the focus of resilience from geographic assembly redundancy to managing a technical transition.

#### 4. The Structured Framework (75/25 Volume Split)
To test both paths objectively, a structured seventy-five/twenty-five volume allocation was implemented for one year alongside several strict parameters:
- **Joint Quality Program:** The secondary supplier must meet predefined quality, delivery, and defect thresholds to maintain its twenty-five percent allocation.
- **Granular Cost Tracking:** All engineering support hours dedicated to the secondary supplier's process are tracked to determine total cost of ownership.
- **Controlled Buffer Stock:** The eight-week local inventory is owned by the manufacturer, with structured procedures to keep firmware updated.
- **Managed Commercial Clauses:** A rolling ninety-day forecast provides visibility, while a restricted \"first opportunity to quote\" clause for the primary supplier includes short response windows to prevent default path dependency and protect competitive development.
- **Component Redesign Path:** To navigate the eighteen-month semiconductor package obsolescence, the manufacturer is funding a shared interface and validation specification. Both suppliers are invited to propose implementations rather than automatically awarding the design to one, preventing a single technical failure path while avoiding the double validation overhead of two entirely distinct systems.

#### 5. Decision Parameters at State
At the twelve-month review, the final sourcing distribution will be decided based on total support cost, actual defect rates, redesign milestones, and proven recovery capabilities rather than abstract arguments or the initial eighteen-percent headline discount.
