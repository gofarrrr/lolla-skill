In evaluating whether to hire a highly celebrated commercial executive into a founder-led company currently experiencing a two-year revenue plateau, leadership must shift from seeking validation of a prestige résumé to executing a rigorous, structured assessment of fit, authority, and operating standards.

### 1. Job Design and Boundary Definition
The primary risk of hiring a high-profile commercial leader is failing to define exactly where her authority ends and where founder/CEO authority begins. To mitigate this:
*   **Draft an Authority Charter:** Establish a formal document approved by the board detailing what she owns (the commercial organization, pricing execution, and leadership changes within an approved budget) relative to reserved CEO decisions (company strategy, major product commitments, and dismissing a founder).
*   **Address Boundary Disputes Directly:** Before she starts, establish a written conflict-resolution process for common grey areas, such as disputed customer promises or product commitments.
*   **Avoid Selective Delegation:** Delegation must be organizational and structured, not a temporary convenience that the founder overrides whenever comfortable patterns are challenged.

### 2. Behavioral Reference Checking
Instead of evaluating only selected and positive references, seek a complete view of how the candidate operates under pressure:
*   **Seek Diverse Perspectives:** Seek references beyond her selected list, including former direct reports who did not stay, peers, and external partners.
*   **Analyze Operational Patterns:** Rather than looking of a binary 'good' or 'bad' verdict, analyze specific operational behaviors. In this case, a former regional director who left after eighteen months pointed out a critical pattern: she would change priorities without explicitly addressing the sacrifices made on the previous plan, leading teams to treat daily commitments as temporary theater. 
*   **Test Self-Correction:** Present this pattern of feedback back to the candidate to see how she responds, communicates costs, and maintains upward feedback lines when plans change.

### 3. Practical Auditing: The Workshop
A hands-on evaluation, such as a two-day paid pricing workshop, is highly effective but must be carefully managed to de-bias the feedback:
*   **Observe Conflict and Communication:** Real-world workshops show how candidate structures problems, frames disagreement, and interacts with established leaders (particularly product/engineering teams who may feel commercial pressure competes with long-term roadmaps).
*   **Separate Workshop Debriefs:** Perform individual de-briefs with internal participants before sharing the founder's perspective. In a highly visible hire, the team is prone to perform for the CEO once they perceive the CEO is impressed, which can distort honest feedback.

### 4. Rewriting Plans and Aligning Incentives
*   **Evaluate Adaptability Over Polished Plans:** The willingness of the candidate to adapt her initial plan based on collaborative feedback (e.g., changing her immediate sixty-day leadership change window to an initial thirty-day diagnostic period) is stronger evidence of operational fit than her original polished proposal.
*   **Durable Incentive Design:** Equity and performance incentives must protect the long-term health of the business to prevent short-term, fragile revenue. Rather than basing rewards on revenue milestones alone, tie vesting to a balanced mix of metrics such as revenue, gross retention, and contribution margin, explicitly barring vesting based on single large, unsupportable contracts.

### 5. Structured Review and Governance
An offer should be made with clear organizational expectations, including a defined six-month review that evaluates both company outcomes and the health of the decision-making pipeline. Records of peer debriefs, reference disagreements, original and revised plans, and the authority charter must be preserved. At the six-month mark, these documents serve to analyze whether the hire succeeded or failed based on structural alignment, founder behavior, or economic conditions, preventing a simplistic 'hero' or ' scapegoat' narrative.
