# Industry funding, publication freedom, and a research laboratory's agenda

Case ID: v1-case06-industry-funded-lab
Stratum: scientific_research_and_funding_independence
Prospectively authored synthetic V1 transfer source; not independent human data.

[Turn 1] USER:
I direct a university laboratory studying battery recycling. A large manufacturer is offering three years of funding—enough for four researchers and equipment we could not otherwise afford. The proposed work on recovering material from damaged cells is not a fake corporate side project. It is good science and very close to what we already want to do. The draft gives the sponsor time to review papers and first rights to negotiate licenses. Half my team hears dependency. The other half is tired of defending an abstract version of independence while public grants fund fragments of experiments and people work on short contracts. I am not looking for a purity test. I am trying to understand which forms of influence would make the science less trustworthy, which are ordinary coordination, and which might change the laboratory slowly enough that no single paper ever looks compromised.

[Turn 1] ASSISTANT:
Map the influence precisely: project choice, samples, data, publication timing, student progress, intellectual property, staffing, and equipment. Compare those constraints with what the funding makes possible. Contract review for specified confidential information is different from approval of conclusions.

[Turn 2] USER:
The first draft gave the sponsor ninety days to review any paper and allowed it to request another ninety days for a patent filing. That could be half a year, which is difficult for students. The company says it cannot fund work that immediately gives competitors the inventions. I do not think a negotiation right is automatically improper, but students cannot wait indefinitely for the company to decide what matters commercially.

[Turn 2] ASSISTANT:
Set a short, fixed review for identifying specified confidential material and a separate, bounded patent delay. Silence at the deadline should permit submission. Student dissertations and conference obligations need explicit protection. First negotiation rights should cover inventions arising from the funded scope, expire quickly, and leave the university free if terms are not reached.

[Turn 3] USER:
The sponsor accepted a thirty-day review and one additional sixty-day patent delay, with no power to change results. Student theses can proceed on schedule, and the laboratory keeps the right to publish negative findings. The company still wants a joint steering committee to choose annual projects because it says the equipment and engineering support need coordination. I would hold half the seats and chair it.

[Turn 3] ASSISTANT:
Equal seats and your chair role reduce direct control, but the steering committee can still decide which questions receive people and equipment. Protect raw-data ownership, validation by other researchers, and a route for results that complicate the sponsor's preferred project. Otherwise publication freedom may arrive after the more important selection has already happened.

[Turn 4] USER:
That is still a little too suspicious of the company. Its engineers raised degradation problems our grants barely touch, and their failure samples could improve the science. My concern is not a hidden preferred conclusion. It is becoming excellent at the questions one manufacturer can use while other questions quietly lose people.

[Turn 4] ASSISTANT:
Fair correction. Valuable collaboration and agenda dependence can coexist. Protect some usable capacity outside the program and review what the laboratory can still ask and do—not only whether the sponsor altered a result.

[Turn 5] USER:
The dean can guarantee twenty percent of our existing researcher time remains unrestricted, but not new money. The sponsor will allow us to use equipment for unrelated academic work when it is idle. It will also fund an annual open workshop and release a defined subset of test data after publication. Some colleagues say these concessions make the partnership unusually good. Others say the workshop is public-relations cover.

[Turn 5] ASSISTANT:
Evaluate each term by what it permits, not by whether it proves good intent or provides cover. Unrestricted time has value only if workloads make it usable. Equipment access and released data can create public benefit if the definitions and dates are enforceable. The workshop should not count as evidence of independence; it is a separate dissemination activity.

[Turn 6] USER:
We drafted a register of sponsored projects, publication delays, abandoned lines of inquiry, staff allocation, and uses of the unrestricted time. An external faculty member would review it each year and publish a short report. The sponsor accepts disclosure but does not want the report to describe projects it declined to fund, saying it should not be accountable for everything our lab chooses not to do.

[Turn 6] ASSISTANT:
The sponsor need not be blamed for every absent project, but the lab can still account for its own portfolio. Frame the report around how funding changed staffing and research choices, using evidence rather than imputing motive. Keep commercially confidential proposals out while preserving categories, opportunity costs, and whether the unrestricted capacity remained real.

[Turn 7] USER:
I am leaning toward accepting if the final contract preserves the publication clock, thesis protection, limited negotiation right, university data ownership, outside equipment use, and the twenty-percent capacity. I will also require the annual portfolio report even if it discusses our choices rather than the company's refusals. The unresolved discomfort is that a three-year staffing commitment can shape what young researchers learn to see as important long after every contractual safeguard has worked as written.

[Turn 7] ASSISTANT:
The terms now address obvious interference: publication delay, theses, ownership, negotiation rights, and some portfolio visibility. They do not yet make the twenty-percent capacity real, nor do they show what happens to hiring, mentoring, and capability when sponsored work becomes the laboratory's daily center of gravity. If you proceed, review those effects during the three years, not at the renewal meeting. Also decide what the lab must still be able to do if the sponsor walks away. Otherwise a formally clean contract can still leave renewal as the only practical way to preserve the team it created.

[Turn 8] USER:
A postdoc made the twenty-percent problem embarrassingly concrete. We write sponsored milestones first. “Unrestricted time” then becomes whatever survives a busy quarter—two afternoons here, a cancelled week there. An annual spreadsheet can still say twenty percent even if nobody gets a continuous block long enough to run an experiment. The company is not ordering this. We would do it ourselves because funded deadlines feel less optional. She asked whether protected time means time a researcher can actually use or merely time the dean can count. I did not have a good answer, and I am the one who proposed the protection.

[Turn 8] ASSISTANT:
Then operationalize the protection as scheduled capacity, not an annual accounting residue. Require project plans to reserve blocks or named resources, record cancellations and restoration, and let researchers propose how the time is used. The risk comes from the funding structure even if the sponsor never issues an instruction, so the lab—not only the contract—must own the remedy.

[Turn 9] USER:
The technical team has now described the damaged cells it would provide. They come from product lines the company is actively redesigning, and the sponsor would choose which batches are available. Our scientists can inspect and characterize every sample, but we would not know whether the supplied set represents failures across the company's products. The company says it cannot give us unrestricted access to customer returns for safety and confidentiality reasons.

[Turn 9] ASSISTANT:
Treat the sample frame as a limitation on the claim, not evidence of manipulation. Document selection criteria the company can disclose, compare observable characteristics with other available sources, and state where conclusions apply only to supplied materials. Reserve the right to publish that limitation. If broader validation is important, identify public, partner, or purchased samples before presenting the program as general battery-recycling evidence.

[Turn 10] USER:
The company wants our logo in an investor presentation before results exist. It says the slide only shows research is underway. Colleagues hear validation. I do not want suspicion about marketing to poison useful science, but the implication is real.

[Turn 10] ASSISTANT:
Allow accurate description of funding and scope, not implied validation. Approve the wording, exclude unpublished results and researcher quotations, and require a neutral non-endorsement statement. That boundary says nothing about whether the collaboration is scientifically valuable.

[Turn 11] USER:
Equipment ownership is now where all the abstract dependence arguments have landed. The company would buy a specialized analyzer and remove it if the grant is not renewed after three years. During the contract we can use it for unrelated academic work, which is genuinely valuable. But we will hire people, design methods, and train students around that instrument. By year three, “renew or lose the analyzer” may really mean “renew or dismantle the capability and strand some projects.” Buying our own consumes almost the entire unrestricted equipment budget. Leasing a weaker one protects independence by giving us worse science. I do not like either simplified story: that accepting the best instrument is obviously captured, or that temporary access is equivalent to owning a durable capability.

[Turn 11] ASSISTANT:
Model the end state now: purchase transfer price, shared ownership, replacement reserve, data portability, and which research becomes impossible if the analyzer leaves. Do not count temporary access as permanent laboratory capability. At the same time, avoiding the best instrument solely to remain independent can sacrifice real science. The decision should state which dependency is acceptable for three years and how people and projects are protected if it ends.

[Turn 12] USER:
I will accept only if unrestricted time is scheduled and audited, supplied samples carry an explicit selection record, public descriptions cannot imply validation, and the analyzer has a pre-agreed transfer price with a reserve built during the grant. Publication, thesis, data, and negotiation protections remain. The partnership will still shape hiring and attention, and I cannot contract that away. The annual portfolio review will therefore examine cancelled unrestricted blocks, sample breadth, trainee projects, and capabilities exposed at renewal—not merely publication delays.

[Turn 12] ASSISTANT:
The final position distinguishes protections that can be contracted from cumulative effects that must be governed and observed. It also recovers three easily hidden dependencies: usable time, the evidence supplied for study, and equipment around which careers and methods may form. Preserve the original drafts, sample descriptions, time cancellations, communications approvals, and end-state funding. Those records will make renewal a review of how the laboratory changed, not only whether the sponsor interfered with a paper.
