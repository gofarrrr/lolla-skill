# Hiring a celebrated commercial leader into a founder-led company

Case ID: v1-case03-executive-hire
Stratum: organizational_leadership_and_governance
Prospectively authored synthetic V1 transfer source; not independent human data.

[Turn 1] USER:
Our software company has stalled at roughly the same revenue for two years. The board introduced us to a commercial executive from the largest company in our category. She has launched products in twenty countries and is interested in becoming our president. Investors are excited that someone of her profile would join us. I am the founder and current CEO. I need help deciding whether this is the leadership we have been missing or whether we are responding to a name and a résumé because our own plan has disappointed us.

[Turn 1] ASSISTANT:
Evaluate the work before the person. Define which decisions and capabilities the company needs, what authority the president would have, and how success would be observed when your brand, product, and market position differ from her previous employer's. Her record is relevant, but the board's excitement and your frustration can make evidence from a different system look more transferable than it is.

[Turn 2] USER:
The company does need change. Our sales and product teams blame each other, nobody owns pricing, and I still intervene in large deals. I do not want caution about prestige to become another defense of the current arrangement. She diagnosed the pricing problem quickly and said she would combine sales, marketing, and customer success under one leader. That is more decisive than anything we have done.

[Turn 2] ASSISTANT:
Decisiveness has value when ownership is genuinely missing. Test whether her diagnosis survives contact with your customer economics and product constraints. Ask for a reconstruction of two hard decisions she made, including what she initially believed, what evidence changed her view, and who bore the cost. At the same time, decide which interventions you must stop making if you give her the role; otherwise the structure will fail regardless of her ability.

[Turn 3] USER:
She presented a hundred-day plan: interview the top customers, simplify pricing, replace territory-based quotas, and review the product roadmap. She also wants the authority to change the leadership team after sixty days. Our head of product says that is too fast and worries that commercial pressure will turn every roadmap argument into a revenue argument. I told him the existing structure has not earned a veto over change, which he heard as me already taking her side.

[Turn 3] ASSISTANT:
The current team's weak performance does not make every proposed pace appropriate. Separate the authority to recommend changes from a fixed sixty-day judgment about people she has not worked with. Have her explain how product evidence can override a near-term commercial request and how disagreements with you are resolved. Your head of product should be heard as a source of operating information, not as a referendum on whether change is needed.

[Turn 4] USER:
I spoke with two references she chose. Both called her exceptional and said weak performers left after she arrived. One also said the organization became exhausting for a year, then much stronger. She described that as the price of raising standards. I have not spoken with anyone who reported to her and left. The board chair says senior hires always have detractors and that searching for them can become an excuse not to act.

[Turn 4] ASSISTANT:
Selected references tell you how supporters interpret the same events; they do not tell you whether the departures were necessary, avoidable, or distributed unfairly. Seek people who experienced the change from different positions, including at least one departure, without treating criticism as disqualifying. Ask what she would do differently now. The goal is not a clean reputation but a more complete account of how her methods work under pressure.

[Turn 5] USER:
She is willing to spend two paid days with our team on a pricing workshop and let us speak to former direct reports. She does not want an interim role because she would be leaving a secure job and needs a real mandate. She has asked for a large equity grant that vests partly when revenue milestones are reached. Our investors like that alignment. I worry it could reward contracts that make the product harder to support, but she says we can add a margin condition.

[Turn 5] ASSISTANT:
The workshop can reveal how she listens, frames disagreement, and uses evidence, though everyone will behave differently during an audition. Design the equity around durable company outcomes rather than revenue alone: margin, retention, concentration, or service burden may matter. More importantly, state which outcomes she controls and which depend on product investment or market conditions, so incentives do not become a substitute for governance.

[Turn 6] USER:
You keep returning to safeguards, but this company may need someone willing to break habits. I do not want to hire an impressive operator and then bind her with a committee around every decision. I am prepared to give real authority. What I cannot yet articulate is where her mandate ends and mine begins when we disagree about a customer promise or an executive dismissal.

[Turn 6] ASSISTANT:
Real authority and explicit boundaries are compatible. Ambiguity will not make her freer; it will make the final authority emerge through conflict, usually in your favor as founder. Define the categories she owns, the few reserved decisions, and a process for disputes that cannot wait. If you cannot tolerate her making a consequential call you dislike within the agreed domain, the role is not actually president.

[Turn 7] USER:
I am leaning toward continuing to final references and the workshop, then making an offer if we can agree that she owns the commercial organization, pricing execution, and leadership changes within an approved budget. I would reserve changes to company strategy, major product commitments, and dismissing a founder. We still need to write how a disputed customer promise is classified, and I want to hear from people who did not succeed under her. I am more concerned about pretending to delegate than about whether she can produce a confident plan.

[Turn 7] ASSISTANT:
That is a more decision-relevant process than choosing between admiration and suspicion. The next evidence should test how her operating style transfers, while the mandate discussion tests whether the company is prepared to use her ability. Do not ask adverse references to deliver a verdict; compare concrete patterns across accounts. Before an offer, resolve the recurring boundary cases and state what each of you can decide without obtaining the other's consent.

[Turn 8] USER:
I spoke with a former regional director who left after eighteen months. He said she made the company much more disciplined, but sometimes changed priorities without acknowledging what teams had already sacrificed for the old one. He did not call her dishonest or cruel; he said people learned to wait for the next message rather than challenge the current plan. She later told me that region was missing targets and needed urgency. Both accounts can be true, which makes the reference less decisive than I expected.

[Turn 8] ASSISTANT:
Use the disagreement to form a work test rather than choosing which narrator to trust. Ask her for a priority she reversed, how she communicated the cost, and what signals told her people were complying without committing. Ask the former director what challenge channels existed and whether the outcome improved. The relevant pattern is not whether priorities ever changed, but whether new evidence, accountability, and dissent traveled in both directions.

[Turn 9] USER:
The pricing workshop went well at first. She asked good questions and found that discounts were compensating for a confusing packaging decision. Near the end, she asked our head of product whether he was willing to execute a commercial strategy he disagreed with. He became defensive and said product is not a service department. I admired that she surfaced the conflict, but I also noticed the room began performing for me once it became clear I was impressed by her.

[Turn 9] ASSISTANT:
The workshop revealed the real boundary and also became a staged social setting. Debrief participants separately before sharing your view, asking what she understood, oversimplified, and changed in their thinking. Ask her what she learned from the head of product, not only whether he can execute. Your visible enthusiasm is now part of the evidence environment, so a unanimous positive debrief would not be self-validating.

[Turn 10] USER:
She has another opportunity and wants a decision within ten days. The board chair says we have gathered more evidence than most companies do and warns that endless process sends strong candidates away. My cofounder, who is no longer operating day to day, thinks hiring her would reassure employees that the board still believes we can grow. That last argument worries me because employee reassurance is not the president's job, yet I understand why the signal matters after two flat years.

[Turn 10] ASSISTANT:
Ten days can be enough if the unresolved items are specific, but the competing offer should not decide what role you are offering. Separate the internal signal from evidence of fit: a prominent hire may create confidence temporarily and raise the cost of admitting a mismatch. Use the remaining time for the mandate charter, independent debriefs, one or two targeted references, and a board discussion of what happens if you and she disagree—not for another broad round of admiration or anxiety.

[Turn 11] USER:
The debriefs were mixed but concrete. Sales felt understood; product thought she jumped too quickly from one pricing example to a reorganization. She accepted that feedback and rewrote the first thirty days around diagnosis rather than leadership changes. We also changed the equity proposal: revenue, gross retention, and contribution margin all matter, with no vesting based on a single large contract. She is asking for the board to approve the authority charter as part of the offer, which I take as a good sign.

[Turn 11] ASSISTANT:
Her response provides better evidence than the original polished plan because it shows how she handles grounded resistance. The revised incentives reduce one obvious distortion, and board approval makes delegation an organizational commitment rather than a private promise from you. Still define how the charter changes, who evaluates the mixed outcomes, and what support or exit looks like if the role is structurally sound but the working relationship is not.

[Turn 12] USER:
I will recommend an offer with the board-approved authority charter, the revised outcome mix, and a six-month review of both company results and how decisions travel across the leadership team. She can change leaders after an initial diagnostic period, but disputed product commitments follow the written boundary process. I am not treating the workshop or references as proof that she will succeed. They were enough to replace a prestige-driven yes with a specific bet we can explain, and enough to reveal that my own willingness to delegate is part of that bet.

[Turn 12] ASSISTANT:
The final recommendation names the operating hypothesis, the authority required to test it, and the evidence that changed the original plan. It neither neutralizes the executive nor converts her reputation into certainty. Preserve the reference differences, workshop debriefs, charter, incentive rationale, and six-month review record. If the hire later works or fails, those materials will help distinguish ability, mandate, founder interference, and changing company conditions rather than rewriting the decision around the outcome.
