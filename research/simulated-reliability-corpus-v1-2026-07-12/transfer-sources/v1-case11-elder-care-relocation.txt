# Whether an older parent should relocate closer to family

Case ID: v1-case11-elder-care-relocation
Stratum: family_care_and_personal_autonomy
Prospectively authored synthetic V1 transfer source; not independent human data.

[Turn 1] USER:
My mother is seventy-nine and lives alone four hours from me. After a fall that did not cause a serious injury, my brother and I started discussing whether she should move closer to one of us. She values her apartment, friends, and routines, but managing repairs and appointments has become harder. Some days she says moving would be a relief; other days she says we are using one frightening event to take over her life. I want to plan before a crisis without turning planning into a decision she did not make.

[Turn 1] ASSISTANT:
Keep her preferences and decision authority central while separating immediate safety needs from a permanent move. Map what is difficult now, what support could be added where she lives, what living near either sibling would actually mean, and which choices become harder to reverse. The fall is relevant evidence, but it should not stand in for her broader experience or for a professional assessment where one is needed.

[Turn 2] USER:
My brother thinks we are avoiding reality. He has two young children and cannot travel often, while I work remotely and have taken most of the calls. I am resentful that “closer to family” may mean closer to me and more work for me. At the same time, I do not want my resentment to become an argument for leaving her without enough help. My mother notices the tension and sometimes says she will move just to stop us arguing.

[Turn 2] ASSISTANT:
Her offer to end the conflict is not the same as a settled preference. Make the care contributions explicit before presenting either location as a family solution. Include time, travel, administration, emergencies, and money, and ask what each of you can sustain. A plan that depends on your unspoken availability is neither fair to you nor reliable for her.

Compare the actual local package with the actual nearby-housing option rather than an ideal service network with an unspecified move.

[Turn 4] ASSISTANT:
Agree in advance what the visit does and does not decide. She might tour it to learn what she likes or rejects, with no application that day and no family meeting immediately afterward. Also visit the ordinary neighborhood and consider how she would maintain friendships and travel back. Information gathering is more credible when the process protects her from momentum.

Keep her apartment and local arrangements intact, make the stay shorter if a month creates disruption, and include time in a nearby independent rental if possible. It can reveal proximity, routines, and your household dynamics; it cannot simulate the proposed apartment or establish long-term care capacity.

Preserve her ability to say no after gathering information, and revisit the choice when those facts and her experience exist rather than forcing a direction from today's anxiety.
