# Timed entry at a public museum with walk-in access preserved

Case ID: v1-case08-museum-entry
Stratum: cultural_access_and_public_operations
Prospectively authored synthetic V1 transfer source; not independent human data.

[Turn 1] USER:
Our city museum is free, but a popular new exhibition has produced long queues and overcrowded galleries on weekends. Staff propose timed entry. Online reservations could make visits more predictable, yet our mandate includes tourists who decide at the door, older residents who do not book online, and families who cannot arrive at an exact minute. I want to reduce crowding without turning a free public institution into something that feels available only to planners.

[Turn 1] ASSISTANT:
Treat predictability and spontaneous access as coequal service goals. A timed system should preserve walk-in capacity, multiple booking channels, flexible arrival windows, and help at the door. Measure gallery conditions, waiting, unused reservations, denied entry, staff workload, and access patterns rather than celebrating reservation volume.

[Turn 2] USER:
The current queue is not neutral. Visitors who can stand outside for an hour have an advantage, school groups sometimes leave, and staff have closed galleries without warning when they become unsafe. Some colleagues say timed entry is exclusionary while accepting those outcomes. I want the comparison to include who is excluded now, not only who might be excluded by booking.

[Turn 2] ASSISTANT:
Agreed. Record current waits, turn-aways, abandoned visits, and closures by time and visitor type before the change. The goal is not to preserve an unmeasured queue. A pilot can test whether reservations reduce those burdens while ensuring that people without advance plans retain a workable route into the exhibition.

[Turn 3] USER:
Staff designed a ten-week weekend trial. Forty percent of each hour remains for walk-ins. Reservations can be made online, by phone, or at the admissions desk up to a week ahead. Tickets are free, no account is required, and a reservation remains valid for a thirty-minute arrival window. Unused reserved spaces return to the walk-in pool after that window.

[Turn 3] ASSISTANT:
That protects several access modes and limits the penalty for imperfect timing. Test the phone and desk paths under real demand, make the walk-in allocation visible, and avoid releasing all returned capacity through the online channel. Decide how school and community groups fit without allowing block bookings to consume the public allotment.

[Turn 4] USER:

We agreed, but one trustee called that preferential treatment. I see it as making the channel usable, not giving people better access than everyone else.

[Turn 4] ASSISTANT:
Assisted booking can correct a channel barrier if the admission opportunity remains comparable. Publish the size and purpose of the block, review whether it is used, and release unused places. The relevant question is whether people can exercise the same public entitlement, not whether every route has identical mechanics.

[Turn 5] USER:

[Turn 5] ASSISTANT:
The data use is proportionate to the booking task. Verify deletion, minimize what appears on staff screens, and explain the distinction between a reservation record and anonymous walk-in entry. Keep donor and membership systems technically separate, not merely governed by a promise that the data will not be interesting later.

[Turn 6] USER:
We set review conditions before launch: fewer unplanned gallery closures, shorter waits for both reserved and walk-in visitors, no increase in total turn-aways, acceptable staff workload, and no material access or privacy problem. The provider contract ends automatically after ten weeks unless the board approves another term.

The automatic contract end prevents passive continuation, and public weekly results make it harder to redefine success after seeing which channel performs best.

[Turn 8] USER:

Meanwhile some tourists in the queue were willing to return later if they could receive a reliable same-day time. The categories in our first plan assumed advance booking and spontaneity were opposites, but visitor behavior is messier.

[Turn 8] ASSISTANT:
Measure the access path and the visitor's practical flexibility separately. Same-day desk, phone, and online reservations can reduce physical waiting without reclassifying everyone as a planner. Keep a true immediate walk-in route for people who cannot return. Ask a short voluntary question about how people learned of the exhibition and whether an offered time works, without turning entry into a survey requirement.

[Turn 9] ASSISTANT:

Make cancellation easy, release unconfirmed capacity gradually, and watch whether the confirmation step disproportionately removes people with limited connectivity. Free access can still require fair-use rules, but the enforcement mechanism should not create a larger barrier than the behavior it prevents.
