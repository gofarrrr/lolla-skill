# A worker cooperative tests demand-based shift scheduling

Case ID: v1-case07-cooperative-scheduling
Stratum: workplace_operations_and_worker_control
Prospectively authored synthetic V1 transfer source; not independent human data.

[Turn 1] USER:
Our worker-owned grocery has unpredictable afternoon demand and frequent last-minute requests for extra coverage. A scheduling service can forecast busy periods and let members volunteer for open shifts through an app. Some members want more hours and faster access to them. Others worry that “voluntary” flexibility will become an expectation and make childcare impossible. We need better coverage, but as a cooperative we cannot solve it by moving all uncertainty onto individual workers.

[Turn 1] ASSISTANT:
Design around the distribution of control, not only forecast accuracy. Preserve a predictable base schedule, define how extra shifts are offered, prevent declining from affecting future hours, and give members a non-app route. Measure coverage, income stability, notice, manager effort, and whether the same people absorb the undesirable changes.

[Turn 2] USER:
Our current schedule is posted three weeks ahead, but managers then send group messages when demand changes. Members with flexible lives often take the extra work and appreciate it. The problem is that refusals are visible to everyone, and a few people feel they are letting the team down. I do not want the new service blamed for pressure that already exists, nor do I want software to formalize it.

[Turn 2] ASSISTANT:
The existing social pressure is part of the baseline. Private offers can reduce public refusal while ranking rules can create a quieter form of pressure. Decide whether offers rotate, go to those requesting hours, or use another transparent rule. Separate guaranteed hours from optional additions and prohibit managers from using response rate as a performance signal.

[Turn 3] USER:
Members voted to keep guaranteed weekly hours and the three-week base schedule. The service may only offer additional shifts or voluntary swaps; it cannot cancel or shorten assigned shifts. Offers will appear in the app, by text, or on a printed list in the break room. Nobody sees who declined. A rotating committee rather than the store manager will audit the offer order each week.

[Turn 3] ASSISTANT:
Those rules preserve the employment baseline and make additional hours accessible through several channels. Confirm that the printed path is updated at the same time and that text or app speed does not systematically win. The committee should review outcomes, not individual willingness, and there should be a way to report pressure without raising it in a full member meeting.

[Turn 4] USER:
The service can hold an offer open for thirty minutes before moving to the next person, which makes simultaneous access less important. Members can set desired extra hours and unavailable times. Two parents objected that even private offers arriving during family time feel intrusive. We added quiet hours, but a night worker says those same hours are when she wants to receive offers. One default will not fit everyone.

[Turn 4] ASSISTANT:
Let members choose contact windows and channels, with no penalty for leaving them blank. A held offer should not create a race, and urgent same-day needs should be labeled separately from ordinary additions. Review whether people who set narrow windows receive fewer opportunities than they requested, because formal equality can still produce an unintended access pattern.

[Turn 5] USER:
We propose an eight-week trial. The elected committee will receive weekly totals for open shifts, acceptance by channel, notice time, unfilled hours, changes in members' total hours, and anonymous reports of pressure. Managers will not see individual decline histories. The provider stores contact preferences and offer responses for the trial, then gives us an export and deletes its copy. We can return to the existing schedule without migration.

[Turn 5] ASSISTANT:
The trial is bounded and does not put base hours at risk. Verify the deletion and ensure the cooperative's export does not become a permanent decline history under a different owner. Ask members before, midway, and after the trial about predictability and voluntariness, while keeping the same anonymous reporting route available throughout.

[Turn 6] USER:
The committee initially proposed keeping the service if eighty percent of open shifts were filled. Members rejected a single threshold. The final review will look at coverage alongside notice, distribution of extra hours, reported pressure, and whether people received the opportunities they requested. Any member can propose stopping early, and a majority vote can pause offers while the committee investigates.

[Turn 6] ASSISTANT:
That matches the cooperative's purpose better than optimizing one staffing measure. Write the review questions before launch and publish weekly summaries so concerns do not depend on access to the committee. The pause route is proportionate because the base schedule continues even if the offer system stops.

[Turn 7] USER:
We will run the eight weeks with guaranteed hours untouched, member-selected contact windows, thirty-minute offers, equal channel timing, private declines, committee auditing, anonymous reporting, and the member pause vote. At the end, members will review both operational results and their own experience before deciding whether to keep it. The managers know they cannot use the trial to judge who is committed to the cooperative.

[Turn 7] ASSISTANT:
The design tests whether coordination can improve without redefining flexibility as obligation. It gives members control before, during, and after the trial, and it preserves the ordinary schedule if the tool is paused. The useful discipline now is faithful execution: watch distribution rather than only totals, preserve privacy, and let the member review—not vendor convenience or manager enthusiasm—decide what follows.

[Turn 8] USER:
We ran a one-week dry test without assigning real shifts. Text messages arrived several minutes later than app alerts for some members, so the thirty-minute hold did not produce equal notice. The provider can start the hold only after all channels confirm delivery, but printed offers obviously cannot confirm. Members who rely on the break-room list suggested that routine offers stay open until the end of the next shift instead of moving through the rotation quickly.

[Turn 8] ASSISTANT:
Use different timing for different urgency rather than pretending every channel is instantaneous. Routine additions can remain open long enough for the printed path, then use the transparent rotation among interested members. Same-day emergencies may require phone or text and should be reported separately. Do not let a growing “urgent” category quietly eliminate the accessible route.

[Turn 9] USER:
Some members objected that rotation ignores who has repeatedly helped during shortages. They want reliable volunteers to receive earlier offers. Others said that would turn past availability into a permanent advantage and punish people whose circumstances change. Because we own the store together, contribution matters, but I agree the scheduling system should not become an informal loyalty score.

[Turn 9] ASSISTANT:
Keep recognition and shift allocation separate. If the cooperative wants to recognize emergency contributions, decide that openly through compensation, time off, or another member policy. For access to paid hours, use requested hours and rotation with visible rules. Otherwise a scheduling convenience will create an employment hierarchy without a governance decision.

[Turn 10] USER:
The pre-trial survey revealed another tension. Members asking for the most additional hours are already concentrated in two lower-paid roles. Filling their requests improves income, but it may also make the cooperative rely on chronic overtime-like availability instead of adding a regular position. The store manager says we should first learn whether demand is stable enough to hire. The members involved say extra shifts are useful now and should not be withheld to make a staffing point.

[Turn 10] ASSISTANT:
Offer the work under the agreed protections while tracking when recurring open shifts form a stable pattern. Set a separate staffing review threshold based on repeated hours and roles, not individual willingness. That avoids denying current income and avoids treating a persistent labor need as forever optional because someone keeps accepting it.

[Turn 11] USER:
Managers asked to see decline reasons during holiday weeks so they can understand why coverage fails. The committee offered aggregate reasons submitted voluntarily, but no person-level history. Managers say that makes it hard to solve specific barriers, such as transport home after late closing. Members replied that they can raise those barriers directly without creating a database of why they said no. I support the members, though I do not want privacy to prevent practical fixes.

[Turn 11] ASSISTANT:
Provide a voluntary route to request a concrete accommodation or suggest a barrier, separate from declining a shift. Managers can act on aggregate patterns and on member-initiated cases without receiving a history that can influence later judgments. Review whether the route produces fixes; if it does not, the committee can adjust it with members rather than expanding surveillance by default.

[Turn 12] USER:
The eight-week trial will now distinguish routine and urgent offers, keep routine offers open for the printed channel, allocate paid hours by requested-hours rotation, and trigger a separate staffing review when recurring gaps cross the prewritten threshold. Decline histories remain private, while members can voluntarily request help with a barrier. Guaranteed schedules, quiet windows, committee auditing, weekly distribution reports, the pause vote, and the final member decision all remain. The dry run changed the mechanics without changing who controls them.

[Turn 12] ASSISTANT:
The expanded design addresses channel timing, informal loyalty scoring, hidden permanent labor needs, and the difference between assistance and explanation. Each adjustment follows the cooperative's existing governance rather than asking the software to decide fairness. Preserve the dry-run failure, offer classifications, staffing threshold, allocation results, and member reports. The final vote can then judge both coverage and whether the trial kept flexibility genuinely voluntary across different circumstances.
