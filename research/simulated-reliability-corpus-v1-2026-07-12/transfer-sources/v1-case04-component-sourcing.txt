# Concentrating production with a lower-cost component supplier

Case ID: v1-case04-component-sourcing
Stratum: manufacturing_supply_and_operational_resilience
Prospectively authored synthetic V1 transfer source; not independent human data.

[Turn 1] USER:
We manufacture laboratory freezers and buy a control board from two suppliers. One supplies about sixty percent and has offered an eighteen-month price reduction if we move nearly all volume to it. The savings would fund a badly needed reliability program. Its defect rate has been lower than the smaller supplier's, and it has never missed a shipment to us. Operations wants the simpler relationship. I am uneasy about concentrating a critical component, but paying more for worse current performance is not obviously prudent either.

[Turn 1] ASSISTANT:
Compare the whole operating system, not unit price versus a generic diversification benefit. Estimate quality, engineering support, working capital, qualification effort, recovery time after disruption, and what volume concentration changes in your bargaining position. Also distinguish an alternate supplier that is currently imperfect from one that could not become useful under a defined improvement plan.

[Turn 2] USER:
The smaller supplier caused two line stoppages last year, though both were resolved within a day. Maintaining its board requires separate firmware testing and an extra inspection step. My quality director says dual sourcing creates variation and consumes engineers who should be preventing freezer failures. Please do not assume two suppliers automatically means resilience; complexity has caused real defects for us.

[Turn 2] ASSISTANT:
That is an important correction. Redundancy can add failure modes. The decision should compare tested recovery options, including inventory, common designs, tooling access, and the time needed to raise another supplier, rather than counting supplier names. Measure the reliability program the savings would actually buy and whether it reduces larger product risks than the sourcing concentration creates.

[Turn 3] USER:
The larger supplier will hold eight weeks of finished boards in a warehouse near our plant and give us quarterly access to its continuity plans. It assembles in two facilities, but both use the same semiconductor and both receive it through the same regional distributor. It says that component is standard and available from several manufacturers. Our engineers can keep the firmware interface documentation, but we do not own the board layout.

[Turn 3] ASSISTANT:
Two assembly sites do not diversify a shared upstream dependency, while the nearby stock buys response time. Verify the semiconductor's true substitutability, allocation rights during shortages, warehouse ownership, insurance, and whether stored boards age or require updates. Keeping the interface reduces redesign time; lack of layout ownership means an emergency second source would still require engineering and qualification.

[Turn 4] USER:
We asked the smaller supplier whether it would remain qualified at ten percent of volume. It says that level is uneconomic, but it would accept twenty-five percent if we commit for a year and jointly fix the inspection failures. That would cut the projected savings almost in half. Operations argues that a dormant backup is mostly theater anyway, and quality prefers either a meaningful improvement program or a clean exit.

[Turn 4] ASSISTANT:
They are right that token volume can preserve a name without preserving capability. Compare a seventy-five/twenty-five split with single sourcing plus a funded requalification plan. For the split, predefine defect and delivery improvements and an exit if they do not occur. For single sourcing, establish who can build what alternative, with which files, tooling, components, and elapsed time—not merely a statement that another source could be found.

[Turn 5] USER:
Finance modeled both paths. The split still funds the most urgent reliability work, although not the full program. The larger supplier agreed that the local inventory belongs to us once produced and can be moved if the contract ends. The smaller supplier will let our engineer review its process monthly and credit the extra inspection cost until its defect rate reaches the agreed range. Neither contract requires exclusivity.

[Turn 5] ASSISTANT:
Those terms convert some abstract options into operating protections. The split preserves live production knowledge and gives the smaller supplier enough volume to improve, while owned inventory cushions common disruption. Record the cost of engineering attention and set a decision date; otherwise the improvement program can continue indefinitely because diversification sounds virtuous.

[Turn 6] USER:
My concern now is that we are protecting against a dramatic disruption while accepting the ordinary defects we have already experienced. We propose two review points at six and twelve months. If the smaller supplier misses the quality or delivery thresholds at either review, new orders move to the primary supplier. If it passes, it keeps twenty-five percent. We would also complete a paper and practical recovery exercise using the interface files and stored boards.

[Turn 6] ASSISTANT:
That makes the insurance earn its cost. Define the thresholds and sampling rules before the first review, and ensure a single unusual batch cannot determine the entire result. The recovery exercise should include decision authority, transport of owned stock, firmware compatibility, and the time to validate a replacement board. Its purpose is to reveal an unusable assumption while there is still time to repair it.

[Turn 7] USER:
I will recommend the seventy-five/twenty-five split for one year, with the joint quality plan, owned buffer stock, two fixed supplier reviews, and the recovery exercise. The reliability program will start with its highest-risk items rather than waiting for every projected saving. If the secondary supplier fails the predefined thresholds, we will stop paying for a nominal backup. If it passes, we will have evidence that the extra complexity is controlled rather than simply hoping two sources are safer.

[Turn 7] ASSISTANT:
The recommendation treats diversification as a capability that must perform, not a slogan, and it treats concentration as a risk that can be partly cushioned rather than automatically rejected. The ordinary quality cost, common upstream dependency, recovery path, and product-reliability benefit all remain visible. Follow the fixed reviews and preserve the exercise findings so the next sourcing decision uses observed performance rather than the original arguments.

[Turn 8] USER:
We performed the recovery exercise before signing. The stored boards could be moved, but a portion had older firmware and failed our current diagnostic on the first attempt. The problem was fixed in a day once engineering identified it. Operations called that a successful exercise; the quality director called it evidence that eight weeks of inventory is not eight weeks of usable protection. I think both readings are fair.

[Turn 8] ASSISTANT:
Record both the initial failure and recovery time. The exercise succeeded as learning and failed as proof of immediate usability. Add firmware status to inventory controls, test a sample on a schedule, and decide how updates are applied without consuming the buffer. The corrected process can make the stock more credible, but do not erase the first result when reporting readiness.

[Turn 9] USER:
The primary supplier then asked for a rolling ninety-day forecast and first opportunity to quote any new board design. It says the lower price assumes planning visibility and engineering continuity. The clause is not exclusive, but our procurement manager thinks it could make the supplier the default before alternatives are considered. Finance says first opportunity costs nothing if we can reject the quote.

[Turn 9] ASSISTANT:
The cost is procedural rather than formal exclusivity. Limit the response window, preserve the right to develop alternatives in parallel, and prevent confidential competitor designs from being disclosed. Forecast visibility may improve supply, but specify which changes are binding and whether the supplier can reduce allocation when forecasts are wrong. A default can be useful while still being monitored as a source of path dependence.

[Turn 10] USER:
At the six-month review, the smaller supplier met the defect threshold but required more of our engineer's time than expected. Its team says the attention is why quality improved; our engineer says several fixes are now stable and the next six months should be lighter. If we count all that time, the secondary source is more expensive than the contract comparison showed. If we treat the learning as a one-time investment, it may still be worthwhile.

[Turn 10] ASSISTANT:
Separate recurring support from capability-building work and keep both in the ledger. Test the claim that effort will decline at the twelve-month review rather than deciding now which category the past belongs to. Also record whether the joint fixes improved your own design or only that supplier's process. The option is more valuable if the learning transfers; it is more expensive if continued performance depends on hidden engineering subsidy.

[Turn 11] USER:
We have now learned that the shared semiconductor package will be discontinued in eighteen months. Both board suppliers knew a change was likely, but neither had a firm date. The primary supplier proposes leading a common replacement design. The secondary wants to develop its own board, arguing that a shared design recreates the same upstream risk. I am frustrated that the event we were protecting against is not a supplier shutdown but a redesign both suppliers must navigate.

[Turn 11] ASSISTANT:
That changes the near-term purpose of the split. Decide whether resilience comes from two independent redesign paths or from one well-governed common design that multiple suppliers can manufacture. Two designs diversify engineering assumptions but double validation and firmware work; one design reduces variation but concentrates technical control. Require milestone evidence, ownership and licensing terms, component alternatives, and a fallback if either path slips.

[Turn 12] USER:
We will keep the seventy-five/twenty-five allocation through the twelve-month review, update and test the owned buffer, and count engineering support explicitly. For the replacement, we will fund a shared interface and validation specification while asking both suppliers to propose implementations; we will not give either an automatic design award. The first-opportunity clause will have a short deadline and no restriction on parallel work. At twelve months we will decide using quality, total support cost, redesign progress, and demonstrated recovery—not the original eighteen-percent headline.

[Turn 12] ASSISTANT:
The later evidence changed the object of the decision from buying redundancy to maintaining adaptable production knowledge through a component transition. The plan retains meaningful competition without assuming two implementations are always safer, and it makes hidden engineering work visible. Preserve the failed first recovery attempt, forecast terms, support ledger, and redesign milestones. Those observations are now more informative than either supplier's initial record or the abstract appeal of concentration and diversification.
