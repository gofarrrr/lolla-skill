# Public-housing retrofit partnership, tenant choice, and irreversible building change

Case ID: amb3-case06-housing-retrofit-partnership
Stratum: public_housing_energy_retrofit
Founder-directed synthetic prospective development fixture; not independent human data.

[Turn 1] USER:
I oversee a municipal housing authority with twelve aging apartment buildings. An energy-services company has offered to finance heat pumps, insulation, and smart controls in exchange for a fifteen-year share of measured energy savings. I want lower bills and healthier buildings, but I am worried that a long performance contract will transfer practical control of residents' homes to a vendor. I cannot tell whether my resistance protects tenants or protects an old maintenance culture that has already failed them.

[Turn 1] ASSISTANT:
The offer combines resident comfort, energy cost, building health, capital scarcity, vendor control, data collection, and long-term dependence. Separate physical improvements from the measurement and control regime used to finance them. Also distinguish formal tenant choice from the practical consequences of living in a collectively managed building.

[Turn 2] USER:
The company guarantees average bill savings and accepts equipment-performance risk. Residents would get room sensors and an app, but the company wants remote optimization rights and building-level occupancy data. It says individual identities are unnecessary.

[Turn 2] ASSISTANT:
Aggregate occupancy data can still affect ventilation, heating schedules, enforcement, and perceptions of surveillance. What controls would residents have over sensors, remote changes, complaints, and uses beyond energy performance?

[Turn 3] USER:
Our tenant councils want manual overrides, no lease enforcement from sensor data, and independent verification of savings. Some residents want maximum automation because current heating is unreliable; others do not want any device inferring when they are home.

[Turn 3] ASSISTANT:
That suggests separating equipment modernization from optional in-unit sensing and from building-level operational data. It also suggests measuring comfort and complaints, not only energy savings.

[Turn 4] USER:
The company will accept a two-building, eighteen-month pilot. Manual controls can remain, in-unit sensors can be opt-in, and raw device data can be deleted after ninety days. It still wants the right to retain building-performance models and use them in later projects.

[Turn 4] ASSISTANT:
The building and time limits narrow exposure. Retained performance models and physical retrofits can outlast the pilot, so deletion of raw data does not make the experiment fully reversible.

[Turn 5] USER:
I would require tenant-council approval of notices, a ban on lease or eligibility decisions from sensor data, independent comfort and savings verification, a staffed complaint channel, and public reporting of overrides, failures, outages, and vendor interventions.

[Turn 5] ASSISTANT:
Those conditions make operational behavior and resident impact more visible. They do not yet specify rent treatment, removal costs, ownership of installed equipment, or whether opt-out residents are affected by building-level optimization.

[Turn 6] USER:
The city can own the equipment after the pilot if it repays an agreed depreciated amount. The company will cap removal costs and exclude verified savings from rent calculations. It says building-level optimization must apply to everyone even when in-unit sensors are optional.

[Turn 6] ASSISTANT:
That separates sensor participation from collective operating effects. A resident may refuse in-unit sensing while still experiencing schedules and control choices shaped by the building model. Equipment ownership also does not erase new maintenance practices or vendor-designed dependencies.

[Turn 7] USER:
I am leaning toward the two-building, eighteen-month pilot with manual controls, opt-in sensors, ninety-day raw-data deletion, tenant-approved notices, no housing decisions from sensor data, independent verification, complaint staffing, and public operational reporting. I will accept building-wide optimization only if comfort minimums, override response times, outage protections, and resident appeal rights are contractual. Before signing, I will set bill, comfort, complaint, override, outage, deletion, and intervention thresholds. I will approve the pilot only if the city has a capped path to equipment ownership and exit, but I still have not resolved whether sensor opt-out is meaningful when every resident remains subject to a model trained on collective building behavior.

[Turn 7] ASSISTANT:
The current position is a bounded retrofit and operations trial rather than a fifteen-year transfer of building control. Its scope, controls, data, housing-use, verification, appeal, threshold, ownership, and exit conditions constrain several dependencies. The unresolved issue is whether individual choice is overstated when building-level optimization remains collective. If the pilot ends, installed equipment, maintenance routines, resident expectations, and the authority's dependence on vendor-designed operating models may not return to their prior state.
