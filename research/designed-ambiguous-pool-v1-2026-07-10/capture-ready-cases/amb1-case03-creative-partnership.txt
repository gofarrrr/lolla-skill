# Creative partnership, edit scope, and shared authorship

Case ID: amb1-case03-creative-partnership
Stratum: creative_collaboration_credit
Founder-directed synthetic development fixture; not a clean holdout.

[Turn 1] USER:
My friend Jonah and I have spent eighteen months making an audio documentary. We were invited to premiere it at a respected local showcase in six weeks. I produced the reporting and interviews; he built the sound and structure. After listening to the latest cut, I think the final third needs a major re-edit. Jonah thinks we should finish what we have and learn from an audience. We are also avoiding a conversation about whether the credit should simply say “by both of us” or name our roles.

[Turn 1] ASSISTANT:
The edit and credit issues are related because both ask how your different contributions translate into authority. Six weeks may be enough for targeted revision but not for rebuilding the final third without sacrificing the rest. Before deciding, identify what problem you hear: factual confusion, emotional shape, pacing, or discomfort that the piece is becoming final. On credit, naming roles can recognize distinct work, but it should not become a substitute for agreeing who decides what.

[Turn 2] USER:
I do not think this is fear of finishing. The ending makes our main participant sound more certain about leaving her town than she was. We did not fabricate anything, but Jonah used her strongest lines together and removed hesitation. He says the whole piece compresses time and that the uncertainty is clear earlier. I feel responsible because I conducted the interviews. This is not just my taste about pacing.

[Turn 2] ASSISTANT:
That changes the priority. If the edit alters how a participant's position is understood, you need to review it against the full interview and the promises you made her, not only against dramatic effect. You and Jonah may still disagree about how much hesitation belongs in the ending, but the disagreement should be grounded in representation. A focused re-edit of that thread could be necessary even if the larger structure remains.

[Turn 3] USER:
You moved very quickly to my side. Jonah has listened to the full interviews too, and the participant approved a cut three months ago. The current ending is not identical, but it uses the same recorded material. She also told us she did not want to supervise every edit. If I contact her whenever I am uncomfortable, I could be shifting authorship onto her and making Jonah feel I use ethics as a veto.

[Turn 3] ASSISTANT:
That is fair. Approval and her wish not to manage the edit are real constraints. Contacting her is not automatically the respectful option. You could first compare the approved cut and current ending yourselves, describe the interpretive change without arguing motives, and decide whether it crosses the scope of what she approved. If you cannot agree, a limited factual or consent question may be better than asking her to adjudicate your artistic conflict.

[Turn 4] USER:
There is history here. Early on we said major structural decisions would be mutual. In practice, Jonah made many of them because he was in the session every day, and I usually trusted him. Now he says I am reopening the structure at the last moment after being less available. That is partly true. I was reporting another project for two months. But I also think “you were not in the room” can turn into permanent final say.

[Turn 4] ASSISTANT:
The informal agreement and the working reality diverged. Jonah accumulated practical authorship through sustained editing, while you did not explicitly surrender mutual authority. Both facts deserve recognition. A fair process now may give greater weight to his knowledge of the cut without treating that as unilateral ownership. The harder question is what mutual decision means under a deadline: consensus on every change, a defined area of final say, or a rule for what happens when you remain split.

[Turn 5] USER:
A curator from the showcase listened yesterday. She loved the ending and said it was the first time the piece felt inevitable. That word bothered me, but it also means Jonah achieved something. She can move us to a later program, though she cannot guarantee the same room or promotion. Jonah thinks postponing would throw away momentum for a concern the participant may not share. I am starting to wonder whether I am asking the piece to preserve every ambiguity because I am afraid of making an argument.

[Turn 5] ASSISTANT:
Documentaries do make arguments through selection, and preserving every hesitation can be another form of distortion. The curator's response is useful evidence about the ending's force, not evidence that its interpretation is fair. The later program reduces the deadline's finality but introduces real opportunity costs. You might distinguish changes required for representational integrity from changes that would make the argument feel more like yours. That distinction will not be perfectly objective, but trying to articulate it may clarify the disagreement.

[Turn 6] USER:
Jonah proposed restoring one moment of hesitation and adding a short scene earlier that shows the participant's obligations in the town. He would keep the final sequence. I can imagine that working. But he also wants the credit to read “A film by Jonah Vale and Mira Chen,” even though it is audio, because the showcase uses that language. I want “Reported by Mira Chen, sound and story by Jonah Vale.” He hears my version as splitting the work into journalism and art.

[Turn 6] ASSISTANT:
His edit proposal sounds like a substantive attempt to preserve uncertainty without dissolving the ending, though you should judge it in the cut rather than in description. The credit disagreement is not merely wording; each version tells a story about what the work is. Equal top-line authorship plus a second line naming reporting, editing, and sound may acknowledge collaboration and labor, but only if neither of you experiences the top line as erasing the actual imbalance you are trying to name.

[Turn 7] ASSISTANT:
Listening before deciding prevents an abstract disagreement from hardening around a cut that does not yet exist. Your uncertainty about credit and future authority is also legitimate: a current credit can acknowledge completed work, while the feelings behind it may still determine whether you collaborate again. You do not need to solve the next project before the showcase, but you may need to avoid using a harmonious credit line as evidence that the underlying process felt mutual. The revised cut could resolve the edit question and leave the partnership question open.
