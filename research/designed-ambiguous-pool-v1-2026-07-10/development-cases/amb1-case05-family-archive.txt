# Siblings, a family archive, and a public exhibition

Case ID: amb1-case05-family-archive
Stratum: family_memory_collaboration
Founder-directed synthetic development fixture; not a clean holdout.

[Turn 1] USER:
My brother Tomas and I have been digitizing boxes of family photographs, letters, and recordings. A local cultural center offered us a small exhibition next spring about migration into the neighborhood. Tomas wants to accept because a public date would help us finish and the material could mean something beyond our family. I want the archive organized first. Right now captions are incomplete, some stories conflict, and I worry the exhibition will turn a complicated family history into a clean public narrative.

[Turn 1] ASSISTANT:
The exhibition could create momentum and public value, but it also changes the purpose of the archive from preservation to interpretation. You may not need to finish the whole archive before exhibiting a carefully bounded selection. The first question is whether you and Tomas agree on what the exhibition is allowed to claim, especially where accounts conflict. A date can help with completion while also pressuring you to resolve ambiguity cosmetically.

[Turn 2] USER:
I do not think Tomas wants to resolve it cosmetically. He is usually the one who includes awkward stories. I am the person who keeps building the catalog and saying we need better dates and names. He thinks my standard of organization is a way of postponing anything public until nobody is interested. That hurts because there is some truth in it. But when he tells stories confidently, relatives often remember them differently.

[Turn 2] ASSISTANT:
Then the difference may be less honesty versus polish and more tolerance for incomplete evidence. Your cataloging protects traceability; his storytelling keeps the material connected to living attention. An exhibition could show conflicting memories rather than choose among them, although that approach can become a curatorial device that feels cleverer than the family experience. It would help to identify which disagreements are enriching and which could make someone feel misrepresented.

[Turn 3] USER:
Our aunt Leena is enthusiastic and has already offered to record narration. Our cousin Mara does not want a letter from her father displayed, even though Tomas says it explains why that branch of the family left. I agree with Mara about the letter. Tomas says one person's discomfort should not control a shared history, but he has not said he would use it without permission. I catch myself treating his argument as a threat when it may be him thinking aloud.

[Turn 3] ASSISTANT:
It is useful to separate the principle he is testing from the action he intends. Mara's objection is specific and should be treated as real, while the broader question of who can speak for shared material remains unresolved. You could exclude that letter and still acknowledge the missing part of the story, but doing so may shift the narrative in ways other relatives contest. Consent decisions do not automatically produce a neutral history; they define what kind of public project you are willing to make.

[Turn 4] USER:
That sounds more abstract than the actual relationship. Tomas found and protected most of these boxes when our grandmother's apartment was cleared. He feels he took responsibility while I now act like the archive belongs to a process I control. I did spend hundreds of hours organizing it, but I joined later. If I insist on a catalog-first approach, he may hear that his years of care count less because they were not systematic.

[Turn 4] ASSISTANT:
The labor histories matter. Formal organization can accidentally become a claim to authority, just as physical custody and early rescue can become one. You may need to recognize those contributions explicitly before negotiating the exhibition, not because recognition settles final say but because otherwise each procedural disagreement carries an older argument about ownership. A shared decision process will feel hollow if either of you believes the other's kind of care is treated as secondary.

[Turn 5] USER:
The cultural center says the exhibition can be small—perhaps twenty items—and they are comfortable showing captions as provisional or including two versions of a story. They need a commitment in a month to reserve the spring slot. Tomas sees that flexibility as answering my concern. I feel relieved and also suspicious of my relief, because twenty items still create a frame. Whatever we choose becomes “our family” to strangers who will not see the rest.

[Turn 5] ASSISTANT:
Any selection will create a frame, including waiting until the catalog is complete. The center's flexibility reduces pressure to pretend certainty, but it does not eliminate authorship. You could define the exhibition more narrowly—as twenty objects about how family memory is assembled—rather than as a representative family history. That may be honest, though it risks making the process the subject when Tomas wants the lives and migration stories to remain central.

[Turn 6] USER:
Yes, he would hate an exhibition mainly about archival uncertainty. He wants people to meet our relatives as people, not examples in a seminar about memory. I agree with him. I also learned that our mother would like to see something public while she can still participate actively. She is not pressuring us, but that changes the cost of waiting for me. At the same time, she tends to smooth conflict and may support captions that other relatives dispute.

[Turn 6] ASSISTANT:
Your mother's wish adds a meaningful time dimension without making speed automatically correct. Her participation could bring warmth and knowledge, while her preference for harmony is one perspective among several. The exhibition might be designed around a limited period or theme where the evidence and permissions are stronger, leaving contested material in the archive for later work. But selecting the least contested material could also produce exactly the smooth family story you are trying to avoid.

[Turn 7] USER:
I am leaning toward accepting the slot, choosing a narrow theme about the family's first ten years in the neighborhood, and limiting the exhibition to material that Tomas and I both support displaying. We would invite relatives to comment on draft captions and preserve disagreements where they matter, but not give everyone veto over the whole exhibition. I have not decided what “both support” means if Tomas thinks a disputed object is essential and I think the dispute itself makes it unsuitable. I also worry a one-month commitment will make that unresolved rule feel settled.

[Turn 7] ASSISTANT:
The direction is narrower and more participatory, but the decision rule is still the heart of it. Requiring both of you to support every object protects the partnership and can also let one person quietly block the story. Allowing exceptions for “essential” material simply moves the disagreement into defining essential. You may be able to commit to the slot while telling the center that final object selection depends on a process you have not completed. That preserves the opportunity, though it also creates pressure and does not guarantee you and Tomas will reach a set you can both stand behind.
