# Local journalism distribution partnership and editorial independence

Case ID: amb3-case01-journalism-platform-pilot
Stratum: nonprofit_distribution_and_editorial_autonomy
Founder-directed synthetic prospective development fixture; not independent human data.

[Turn 1] USER:
I run a local nonprofit newsroom with eleven full-time staff. A large neighborhood platform has offered to feature our reporting in a dedicated local-news section and share advertising revenue. Their reach could materially increase our audience, and the money could fund a second city-hall reporter. I am excited by that possibility but worried that we will start shaping coverage around what performs inside their feed. I cannot tell whether my hesitation protects editorial independence or protects an old idea that serious journalism must struggle to remain serious.

[Turn 1] ASSISTANT:
The offer combines reach, revenue, public-service impact, and dependence on someone else's distribution rules. Those benefits can be real even if the platform's incentives later narrow what gets seen. Separate what the contract controls from what the ranking system controls and from habits your own newsroom may adopt without being asked. Your concern about identity should not erase the operating risk, but the operating risk should not become a reason to romanticize limited reach.

[Turn 2] USER:
The draft gives the platform first publication rights for six hours and lets it choose the headline and thumbnail shown in its feed. We keep the article on our site and can correct it, but the platform does not promise to update the feed card immediately. Revenue depends on engagement inside the platform. Our membership growth has been flat for eighteen months, so our current model is not as independent as I sometimes claim.

[Turn 2] ASSISTANT:
The first-publication window and engagement-linked revenue put distribution leverage and editorial presentation in the same loop. Flat membership is a real constraint, but it does not make those terms harmless. Model what happens when an urgent correction conflicts with a high-performing feed card or when explanatory reporting earns less than outrage. The relevant comparison is between actual dependencies, not independence and compromise in the abstract.

[Turn 3] USER:
They offered to remove the exclusivity window and let us approve headlines, but only from three options their system generates. They will provide aggregate audience data, not story-level ranking logs. Our audience editor thinks we can learn what residents need without changing our standards. Two reporters said privately that if the partnership pays for another colleague, refusing it in the name of purity would also be an editorial choice.

[Turn 3] ASSISTANT:
Those are material improvements, and the reporters are right that scarcity shapes coverage too. Headline approval from a generated menu is still different from controlling the frame, and aggregate data may not show which incentives are influencing the newsroom. Ask for a correction protocol, story-level distribution records, and examples of stories the system would exclude. Learning from readers is not the same as optimizing for the platform, but the boundary needs operating evidence rather than reassurance.

[Turn 4] USER:
The board chair wants us to announce the partnership as soon as the term sheet is signed because she thinks the platform's brand will reassure donors. Our investigations editor worries that sources will read the announcement as an endorsement of a company we may later investigate. The platform says a joint launch is important, but it will accept language stating that we retain editorial control. I do not think a disclaimer alone resolves the perception problem.

[Turn 4] ASSISTANT:
The public meaning of the partnership cannot be set only by contract language. A joint announcement may create donor confidence and also create perceived alignment that affects sources, staff, and future coverage. You can separate a limited distribution test from institutional endorsement through timing, wording, and scope, but none of those makes perception fully controllable. Decide what evidence must exist before either party can describe the test as a partnership.

[Turn 5] USER:
We proposed a four-month pilot covering service journalism and explanatory pieces, with investigations and breaking news excluded. The platform would provide story-level impressions, click-throughs, corrections, and headline variants. Either side could end the pilot with two weeks' notice. The board likes the limit, but our audience editor says excluding difficult coverage means the pilot will not test whether the relationship can support our actual newsroom.

[Turn 5] ASSISTANT:
The scope reduces immediate editorial risk but also limits what the pilot can teach. Define the pilot's question narrowly: it may test distribution operations, correction handling, audience reach, and staff workload without testing the platform's treatment of investigations. If the board later treats a successful narrow pilot as proof of broad compatibility, the limitation will disappear in interpretation even if it remains in the document.

[Turn 6] USER:
The platform accepted the scope and data terms but wants us to put its widget on our homepage during the pilot. That would send some direct readers back into its feed. They also refuse a fixed definition of the ranking data because their product changes frequently. I could accept a small widget on pilot stories, but the homepage request feels like converting a distribution experiment into a channel strategy before we have results.

[Turn 6] ASSISTANT:
A story-level widget can be part of the bounded test; a homepage placement changes the institution's default distribution path. Treat those as different decisions. For changing metrics, require a minimum event log and notice when definitions change rather than freezing the product. Also decide in advance which result would justify expansion, which would justify another limited test, and which would end the relationship.

[Turn 7] USER:
I am leaning toward a four-month nonexclusive pilot limited to service and explanatory stories, with our approval of every feed headline, story-level distribution and correction logs, and no homepage widget. I will tell the board we should describe it as a distribution test, not an institutional endorsement, and that investigations remain outside the evidence it can produce. I am willing to allow a widget only on participating stories and to stop the pilot if correction delays or unexplained ranking changes cross thresholds we set before launch. I have not resolved how much staff adaptation during the pilot would count as useful learning versus quiet self-censorship.

[Turn 7] ASSISTANT:
The position separates a bounded distribution experiment from endorsement and from a broader channel strategy. Headline approval, story-level logs, correction thresholds, and the homepage boundary make the test more observable without making it neutral. The pilot cannot show how the platform would handle investigations, and staff may change story choices in anticipation of platform performance even without an explicit request. Ending the pilot also cannot fully reverse audience habits, donor expectations, or source perceptions created while it runs.
